Detection Process
Satellite imagery can detect clouds and weather features between weather reporting stations.
Even though all satellite systems view the Earth from orbit they do so in different manners with
differing coverage and resolutions. Satellite sensors scan the Earth and atmosphere for
electromagnetic radiation (EMR). This includes visible, infrared and microwave radiation.
Systems onboard the satellite digitize this EMR data to radio waves and then transmit the radio
waves to forecast centers to be processed into different satellite imagery.
Imagery Types
Visible (VIS) Imagery
Visible imagery senses reflected, visible radiation. It is only available during the daytime
because it detects the illumination (brightness) of objects. The amount of illumination is
dependent upon four factors: the time of day, time of year, latitude, and cloud heights.
Infrared (IR) Imagery
Infrared sensors detect emitted short and long-wave infrared radiation. IR imagery is available 24
hours a day and includes the temperature of Earth’s surface, which varies diurnally. During
daylight hours land is typically warmer than water, so land will appear darker. Conversely,
during nighttime hours water is typically warmer than land, so water will appear darker.
Water Vapor (WV) Imagery
Water vapor imagery measures mid-range infrared radiation. Cold temperatures will appear
black, while warm temperatures will appear white. Black and darker gray shades depict dry air.
Conversely, white and lighter gray shades are assumed to be moist air. Because WV imagery
falls on the infrared spectrum, it is available 24 hours a day.
Interpretation Considerations
When viewing satellite imagery, there are several factors that have an influence on the
interpretation of the product. Different imagery types are best suited to identify different weather
features and conditions. IR imagery is best utilized to identify high clouds due to their colder
temperatures. Visible imagery works best when identifying low clouds due to the water droplets
in low clouds having higher albedo than the ice crystals in high clouds. WV imagery best detects

Non-cloud Features
Surface Features
These features do not move during image animation and are most evident under clear skies.
Both visible (VIS) and infrared (IR) imagery use various shades of gray to differentiate between
features, whether meteorological or non-meteorological.
Atmospheric Features
Dust and Sand. Areas of dust and sand appear in various shapes and sizes and will have a sharp
upstream edge (source region) and a diffuse downstream edge. In visible imagery, they appear
light to medium gray while on infrared imagery they are virtually invisible without enhancement.
That is because there is little to no temperature contrast.
Smoke and Volcanic Ash. Smoke and volcanic ash is plume-shaped (thin line that broadens out
downstream). They have a light gray appearance on VIS imagery and appear differently on IR
imagery. When visible, smoke has a dark gray color. Although, smoke is normally invisible due
to IR resolution and contamination limitations and a lack of temperature contrast. Volcanic ash
is always visible on IR imagery in a light gray to white color. It may also look like cirrus clouds.
Ship Trails. Anomalous cloud lines may be ship trails formed from a ship’s exhaust. They occur
most often in persistent maritime anticyclones and may last for days. They are bright white on
VIS imagery and virtually invisible on IR due to little or no temperature contrast. If ship trails
are on IR, they have a medium gray color.
Contrails. Contrails are small, white cloud lines formed from an aircraft’s exhaust. On VIS
imagery they may cast a shadow on lower clouds while they are virtually invisible without an
enhancement in IR imagery.

Cloud Features
A cloud is a visible accumulation of minute water droplets and/or ice particles suspended in the
atmosphere above the Earth's surface. We separate clouds by the height of the cloud's base using
three étages: low, middle, and high.

Low étage – clouds lower than 7,000 feet
Cumulus (CU): Cumulus clouds are small to medium sized with slight vertical extent. They are
popcorn-shaped and generally have a bright white appearance.
On VIS imagery, cumulus clouds are small white circular elements in an unorganized cloud
field. They generally have a uniform appearance.
Towering cumulus (TCU): Towering cumulus clouds are medium to large with a moderate vertical
extent. They resemble columns or tower that are widely spaced apart and their tops and sides are
bright white with gray bases.

## Page 4

Towering cumulus also appears as white circular cloud shapes on VIS imagery. The cloud
element is like cumulus, but larger. On IR imagery towering cumulus appears lighter gray to
white.
Cumulonimbus (CB): Cumulonimbus clouds are large to huge with great vertical extent. Their tops
vary in shape; they can be cauliflower or anvil shaped. The tops and sides are bright white with very
dark gray to black bases. In some cases, cumulonimbus clouds have pouches hanging underneath
the cloud’s base. When that occurs, its name changes to Cumulonimbus Mammatus. This is the
easiest cloud element to identify.
It has a large circular shape (bigger than towering cumulus), and it is bright white on VIS
imagery. It may have an anvil plume with a sharp upstream edge that becomes thin and diffuse
downstream. It may also have an overshooting top with small bulges or protrusions centered
above the anvil. Cumulonimbus often casts shadows on lower clouds making it easier to detect
the tallest cloud tops. On IR imagery, cumulonimbus clouds appear bright white without any
enhancement and could have small areas of color indicating the location of overshooting tops.
Fog / Stratus (FG / ST): Fog / Stratus clouds are wide with diffuse edges, and they form in a
uniform sheet with no vertical extent. They appear light gray or medium gray color.
Fog and stratus appear the best on VIS imagery. On VIS imagery, fog and stratus appear as a
white, unform sheet. They have sharp outlines without any texture or lumps There are some
differences between the two. For example, fog conforms to terrain features. It also shrinks or

Middle étage - clouds between 7,000 feet and 18,000 feet
Altocumulus (AC): Altocumulus clouds resemble stratocumulus clouds, but the individual elements
are much smaller. They are widely spaced and circular in shape with little to no vertical extent and
are "dirty" white to light gray.
On VIS imagery altocumulus is a white, non-uniform cloud shield. It has a lumpy texture and
individual cloud elements are hard to see. It looks like stratocumulus, but the texture is more
visible, and altocumulus is brighter.

Altostratus (AS): Altostratus clouds form in a continuous, uniform sheet with diffuse edges and they
normally cover the entire celestial dome. They have no vertical extent, and their appearance is light
gray.
Altostratus and nimbostratus both appear light gray to white on VIS imagery and have a uniform
cloud field with little to no texture.
Nimbostratus (NS): Nimbostratus is a thick, dark gray cloud with little to moderate vertical extent.
The vertical extent can be misleading since it is different from the other clouds. Nimbostratus forms
in the middle étage, but as it develops the cloud's base lowers towards the Earth's surface.
Oftentimes it lowers into the low étage.

Nimbostratus appear lighter gray to white on VIS imagery. They have a uniform cloud field with
little to no texture.
High étage – clouds higher than 18,000 feet.
Cirrus (CI): Cirrus clouds are thin and wispy forming in hooks or filaments. They have no vertical
extent and are bright white.
Cirrus has a milky white appearance on VIS imagery. Thin cirrus is usually invisible due to
contamination while dense cirrus may cast shadows on lower clouds. Regardless, cirrus forms in
streaks or bands. It is bright white on IR imagery. Both thin and dense cirrus are noticeable.

Hemispheric Features
Baroclinic Leaf. Baroclinic leaves are light gray to white on VIS and IR imagery. The bright colors
are typically a cirrostratus cloud shield that hides any other cloud elements. The baroclinic leaf
normally has a shallow S-shape that may or may not have a V-notch in the trailing edge. The
baroclinic leaf typically forms near the base of a long-wave trough.

A comma cloud is light gray to bright white on both VIS and IR imagery. Comma clouds will
always be found downstream of the long-wave trough axis. There are several parts of the comma
cloud.
Surge Region: This is the upstream edge of the comma cloud. The surge region is the point
where the fastest winds intersect with the comma cloud, typically making it a narrow cloud-free
line.
Comma Tail: The comma tail is a long narrow band of cumulonimbus and towering cumulus
clouds extending equatorward. The upstream edge is the vorticity comma cloud, and the
downstream cirrus shield is the baroclinic zone.
Cusp: The cusp is the upstream edge of the comma cloud. It typically has a cyclonically oriented
circular shape.

Baroclinic Zone: The baroclinic zone is in the northeast quadrant of the comma cloud. Best
viewed on VIS imagery, it is the area of the bright white cirrostratus before the clouds begin to
separate and become more diffuse and fragmented along the leading edge of the comma cloud.
Deformation Zone: The Deformation Zone is the extreme poleward side of the entire comma
cloud.
Long-wave trough

## Page 10

The upstream side of a long-wave trough is generally cloud free. Cyclonically orientated, cirrus
streaks form parallel to the wind flow outlining a long-wave trough. Analyze the trough axis in
the center of the greatest cyclonic turning. Other indicators include a baroclinic leaf and comma
cloud. The baroclinic leaf forms near the base of a long-wave trough and comma clouds are
always downstream of the long-wave trough axis.
Long-wave ridge
The downstream side of a long-wave ridge is typically cloud free. Anticyclonically orientated,
cirrus streaks form parallel to the wind flow outlining a long-wave ridge. Analyze the ridge axis
in the center of the greatest anticyclonic turning. Additionally, comma clouds are always
upstream of a long-wave ridge axis.
Polar Front Jet
When analyzing for the Polar Front Jet (PFJ) on satellite imagery place the jet axis in the center
of a baroclinic leaf’s V-notch or nearly center mass of a baroclinic leaf with a well-defined S-
shape. It also applies to the center of the U-shape associated with the surge region of a comma
cloud. The Polar Front Jet is easiest to identify using Water Vapor imagery by observing the
boundary between the dark (dry) and bright (moist) regions in the middle and upper troposphere.

Synoptic Scale Features
Major short-wave troughs
The areas where satellite imagery can help locate major short-wave troughs primarily due to the
divergence and upward vertical motion downstream of the trough axis. The first is on the
upstream side of TCU or CB clouds. The S-shape of a baroclinic leaf is the second instance. The
trough axis is slightly upstream of the cloud mass center.
Major short-wave ridges
Short-wave ridges are normally cloud free. Satellite imagery may have anticyclonically oriented
cloud elements. A major short-wave ridge may be present there, but it is always best to verify it
with another product. Water vapor imagery or animated imagery are best for verifying the
location of major short-wave ridges.
Low height centers
In a comma cloud, the low center is located at the cusp. With a cut-off low the image’s
appearance varies; it can be nearly cloud free or it can have a comma shape. Either way, there
will be noticeable cyclonic rotation in the cloud field.

High height centers
High height centers are normally cloud free. There may be a C-shape that appears on the western
edge of the high center. The best image for identifying high height centers and the C-shape is
water vapor imagery.
Exercise / Practice: Upper Level Features
Low Pressure Center
Satellite images display non-occluded and occluded low-pressure systems. They have large
cloud masses that typically obscure the low center.
Non-occluded system
A baroclinic leaf represents a non-occluded low-pressure system also known as an unstable
wave. The low center is slightly downstream of the baroclinic leaf’s center between the cloud’s
S-inflection point and its easternmost (leading) edge. A cold front is on the equatorward side of
the cloud mass extending from the low center to the trailing edge of the S-shape. A warm front
is also on the equatorward side of the cloud mass extending from the low center to the leading
edge of the S-shape.
Occluded system
Cold fronts transition through the comma tail extending from the triple point to the end of the
comma tail. The inactive cold front begins at the triple point behind the comma tail. It changes
to an active cold front where the comma tail bends the most. The active front then extends to the
front of the comma tail before ending at the end of the comma cloud.

The baroclinic zone often obscures the warm front. The front extends downstream from the
triple point to the farthest eastern portion of the cloud shield. If present, the warm front ends at a
V-notch or wedge in the leading edge of the baroclinic zone.
Comma clouds are associated with occluded systems whether it is a mature or decaying wave.
The low-pressure center location is between the surge region and the cusp for both systems. For
mature waves, it is closer to the surge region since the system is still baroclinic. Decaying waves
are barotropic, so the low center is closer to the cusp.
The occluded front extends upstream from the triple point along the bottom edge of the comma
head. Stationary fronts are the most difficult to locate. They are on the equatorward side of a
fragmented comma tail. The front extends upstream from an active cold front and may continue
along the center of a rope cloud if present. Rope clouds are narrow lines of cumulus or towering
cumulus clouds that form over water.

High pressure centers
The downward vertical motion associated with a high-pressure center dissipates any clouds
making it difficult to locate the center. However, if clouds are present, you will most likely see
Cumulus, Towering Cumulus, and Cumulonimbus clouds southwest of the high, and fog or
stratus to the southeast of the high. Poleward of the high will usually be clear or have thin mid to
high level clouds.
