BLK II - UNIT 4: METOC Workplace Operations
a. METOC Workplace Operations
The goal of the PC will be to show you have the capability to follow guidance and use critical
thinking skills to simulate a day in the life of an operational METOC Forecaster. To successfully
demonstrate competency in this objective, you will be given a series of questions and tasks
designed to display your ability to communicate via phone and email correctly, as well as take
into consideration the operational impacts and potential disruptions to daily tasks. For the
remainder of this objective, follow your instructor’s guidance as they provide instruction for
completing the requirements for this objective.
Professional Workplace Communication
Phone Etiquette
While on shift, there will be times that you will be required to answer the phone. When
answering the phone, you should always try have something to write with and something
to write on in case the call is important enough to need to write down information. You
should also keep the phone call to business/military conversations only. DO NOT use
military phones for personal use. When answering the phone open with a greeting, your
location, your name & rank.
Receiving Calls:
1. Ensure the phone is always on, serviceable, and in working order.
2. Always have a pen and paper ready, to write down pertinent information.
3. Try to answer the phone within the first 3 rings.
4. Respond to the caller by first stating your organization, rank and last name.
5. Be polite when answering calls. Do not speak loudly and avoid responding with the
following mannerisms:
a. Distress
b. Sarcasm
c. Tiredness
d. Exertion
6. Listen carefully to all words of the caller. Pay attention to any clues or specific
details in the message, write them down if you need to.
a. If you do not understand things in the conversation, do not hesitate to ask
them to repeat the information.
7. Summarize what the caller has said using your own words. If you must repeat any
information back verbatim, do so in a polite manner.

Placing Calls
1. Have the full number prepared and ready to dial, either by memory or written down.
a. Instructors should provide you with a contact number during lab days.
2. For local numbers on Keesler AFB that begin with 376 or 377, you do not need to use
any area code.
3. If after 5 rings, usually the individual(s) are away from the phone or are busy doing
other things. You can either try calling again later or continue calling if it is an urgent
matter.
4. If contact is made with the individual you are seeking, offer a greeting that contains
your basic information. Maintain a polite and professional manner during the entire
conversation.
5. There is a possibility you may get put on hold. Sometimes it may last for longer than
usual, often with background music. Be respectful and ready to respond when they
get back to the phone call.
6. Any technical issues that arise from the phones, to include outages, please inform
your instructor.

Email Etiquette
Like phone calls, there will be times while you are on duty that you will be required to send
emails through Outlook. When conducting communications through email it is important to
remain as professional as you do over the phone. Unlike phone calls, you will have more time to
think through what you want to say and how you want to say it.
Microsoft Outlook Basics
Microsoft Outlook is the official email used in the Air Force. It is important to familiarize
yourself with the program and understand the basics. In Outlook, there will be different folders
to choose from, like any other email program.
TO: When you begin a new email, you can insert the email of the person(s) you are sending an
email to, or you can search the Global address book by hitting the “To” button.
CC: If you need to keep certain people informed of your email traffic / information given, but
they are not the primary recipient of the email, you can add them to the CC Block of the email.
SUBJECT: You will always include a Subject when composing an email. Be sure to make this
line as concise and informational as you can to ensure the person receiving the email can
understand why they are receiving this email prior to opening it up.
BODY: In the body of the email, you will begin with a greeting to the person(s) you are
emailing. When emailing a single person always includes their rank or title and their name
(Mr./Mrs./MSgt/Captain Last Name). When emailing a group of people, be professional while
including everyone (ALCON, 335
th Bulls, Team, etc.).
As you end your email attach your signature block to every email to ensure the person(s)
receiving the email have contact information for you in case they need to contact you again. If
you have attachments in your email, ensure you attach all files necessary before sending.

Duty Checklists
As you continue working through your shifts, most METOC locations will have an electronic
checklist that has tasks for you to complete throughout your shift. There will be a spot for you to
put your initials once you complete a task. This is being done to help manage your time and
ensure all required tasks are completed, and as accountability if anyone needs to check which
forecaster completed certain tasks if there is an error or if any clarification is needed.
When completing the checklist:
• Do not pencil-whip the tasks or sign off on them without completing or verifying them.
• Do not wait until the last minute to complete a task. Give yourself enough time.
• Do not complete tasks unreasonably ahead of the prescribed time.
• Always sign off on the task once it is completed, and close to the prescribed time.
• Do not skip tasks, follow them in the listed order.
• Keep the checklist open on a monitor or tab somewhere throughout your shift.
• If you cannot perform the task for whatever reason, you should delegate to another
coworker or document the reason why that task cannot be completed.

Duty Priorities
For each assigned weather assignment, there will be specific duty priorities posted that you
should be familiar with. When you have two or more duties that need to be accomplished at the
same time, you can use the duty priority list to complete tasks in order of importance. Despite
pressure from your leadership, tasks of higher priority must be completed first. Failure to do so

your location at the same time. You must evacuate the building first, before being able to
respond to the in-flight emergency.

Workplace Operation Impacts & P.A.C.E
P.A.C.E. Planning
The P.A.C.E. framework was developed to ensure there is no single point of failure in any
military operation. This framework is used in nearly every aspect of military operations from
communication networks, aircraft missions, and training exercises. The goal of this section is to
identify each phase of a P.A.C.E. plan, and where each phase would be used in a scenario.

The Primary plan is the standard way your unit operates. This plan accounts for multitudes of
variables and remains viable so long as any alterations to the plan are minor. There should be
little to no risk involved in the primary plan, any risks will be well mitigated.
The Alternate plan is the “second best” option for the unit or operation in question.
The
Alternate plan is expected to accomplish mission priorities from the primary plan and is only put
into effect when an unexpected event takes place, requiring a shift in operations that result in the
primary plan being unusable.
The Contingency plan is the third option for the unit or operation in question, and is reserved to
account for large, unexpected changes to both the primary and alternate plans. The contingency
plan is expected to accomplish mission priorities where able and eliminate risk where able.
The Emergency plan is the “last resort” for the unit or operation in question. This plan is
reserved for instances where accomplishing mission priorities or saving resources is the most
important task at hand. The emergency plan is only exercised if the primary, alternate, and
contingency plans have all been rendered unusable. Risk is inherently high with the emergency
plan and should only be used if necessary.
PACE Example:
Communications Mission: Sending a message to a friend.
1. Primary: Send them a text message
2. Alternate: Call them

3. Contingency: Contact someone who knows how to get ahold of them
4. Emergency: Physically travel to the friend to relay the message in person
Notice the primary plan is easy, quick, and effective, making the primary plan the best option for
the mission.
In this scenario, assume you attempt to text, but someone changed the language of your keyboard
to a language you do not speak. Texting your friend is no longer an option, so you must use an
alternate plan to get ahold of them. Calling your friend accomplishes the same goal, with minor
setbacks and inconveniences but is the next best option for accomplishing the mission.
Next, assume that you call but their phone goes straight to voicemail. You must now enact the
contingency plan. You would contact someone who is usually aware of the location and/or
activities of your friend. You contact that person, but they have not heard from your friend in a
few days. The contingency plan has now failed, and you must take emergency action to
accomplish the mission by whatever means necessary.
You decide to physically travel to their house and make direct contact with them. You get the
message to them making your emergency plan the best and last resort option to complete the
mission.
