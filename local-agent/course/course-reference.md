Introduction to Atmospheric Physics and Dynamics
Before you can analyze weather patterns, you need to understand how the atmosphere flows and
circulates. This section introduces the foundational principles that govern air movement,
temperature, pressure, moisture, and stability - the building blocks of meteorology. You will
explore how different layers of the atmosphere interact with each other to influence weather
patterns on a global scale. By mastering these fundamentals, you will gain the tools needed to
analyze current weather patterns, anticipate environmental shifts, and eventually predict the
future state of the atmosphere.

Understanding where weather is and where it is going starts with understanding cardinal

weather features.

Relativity
Relativity is the ability to look at the relationship and interaction between two values or features
without any other outside comparison or fixed standard. Understanding relativity allows you to
interpret temperature, pressure, and moisture differences across different regions. For example, if
you are trying to compare temperature differences, you say that 40 degrees C is cooler than 70 degrees C but
warmer than 30 degrees C. While 40 degrees C might not be considered "hot," relative to 30 degrees C it is warmer, and
relative to 70 degrees C it is cooler.

Wind Flow and Direction
Wind is the horizontal and vertical movement of air, driven by pressure differences and
influenced by terrain, temperature, and atmospheric structure. Air is evaluated by looking at
individual air parcels and how they move horizontally with the wind flow, and vertically within
columns of air from the Earth's surface to the top of the atmosphere. As air parcels travel, their
size will vary based on the temperature and pressure changes relative to each individual air
parcel. Wind terminology provides the language to communicate both the origin and destination
of air movement, helping forecasters interpret charts, anticipate upstream impacts, and convey
conditions clearly and effectively in meteorological contexts.

The suffix -ly indicates wind origin. The suffix -ward indicates wind destination.

Wind Speed
Wind speed in the United States is measured in knots (KT) Other units used to measure wind
speed in various places around the world are meters per second (m/s), kilometers per hour
(km/h), miles per hour (mph). 1 KT is equal to 1.15 mph.

forecasters determine where weather is coming from and where it's headed. This information is
critical when anticipating impacts, planning operations, and issuing weather warnings. These
terms are always relative to your position and the direction of atmospheric flow.

Atmospheric Pressure Fundamentals
Atmospheric pressure is the force exerted by the weight of the air above a given point. It
influences wind, vertical motion, and the behavior of air parcels. Understanding pressure systems
and how parcels respond to them is essential for forecasting.

Parcel Stability and Vertical Motion
Stability determines whether an air parcel will rise, sink, or remain in place. This affects cloud
formation and storm development. Temperature will affect an air parcels stability more than
anything. Warm air is more unstable than cold air. Unstable warm air rises, while stable colder
air will sink. Moisture is another factor in determining parcel stability. Moist air is more
unstable than dry air and therefore more likely to rise. Sinking air parcels are considered stable,
while rising air parcels are considered unstable. The rate at which parcels rise indicate the
amount of instability in a column of air.

Parcel Type Stability Level
Warm/Moist Most Unstable
Warm/Dry Unstable
Cold/Moist Stable
Cold/Dry Most Stable
Atmospheric Heating and Cooling
Heating and cooling processes drive daily temperature cycles and vertical motion in the
atmosphere. These energy exchanges shape parcel behavior and influence weather intensity,
especially in the boundary layer.
Insolation & Eradiation
- Insolation: Solar radiation absorbed by Earth during the day
- Radiation: Heat released back into the atmosphere at night
- Maximum temperature: 1400-1600L (2-4 hours after midday)
- Minimum temperature: 0500-0700L (around sunrise)
Conduction & Diabatic Temperature Change
Diabatic temperature change describes the exchange of heat between air parcels due to
conduction (direct contact) as they move over relatively warmer or colder surfaces. Warm air
parcels will always release heat to become colder, and colder air parcels will always absorb heat
to become warmer.

Convection & Adiabatic Temperature Change
Convection describes the vertical temperature change of an air parcel that will either sink toward
the surface as it becomes more stable or rise away from the surface as it becomes more unstable.
becomes warmer than the surrounding environment and rises away from the surface. The average
vertical temperature change, or "lapse rate" is 2 degrees C per 1000 ft.
Adiabatic Cooling: Adiabatic cooling refers to the process by which a rising air parcel releases
heat into the atmosphere and decreases in temperature until it reaches equilibrium.
Adiabatic Warming: Adiabatic cooling refers to the process by which a sinking air parcel absorbs
heat in the atmosphere and increases in temperature until it reaches equilibrium.
Process Direction Heat Exchange Result
Adiabatic Cooling Rising Releases heat Parcel cools
Adiabatic Warming Sinking Absorbs heat Parcel warms
- Cold air parcels absorbs heat, become more unstable, rise away from the surface, and
begin adiabatically cooling
- Warm air parcels release heat, become more stable, sink toward the surface, and begin
adiabatically warming.

Introduction to Weather Analysis Charts
To display atmospheric conditions, the weather community relies on analysis charts populated
with station plots. These plots present wind flow, temperature, dew point, pressure, and height at
specific locations for various constant pressure levels. Standard weather charts include the
300mb, 500mb, 700mb, 850mb, Surface, and 1000-500mb thickness. and Surface charts. It's
crucial to remember that these pressure levels are not static; they shift seasonally, expanding and
rising during the warmer months due to atmospheric heating, and contracting and lowering
during the colder months.
Atmospheric Heights
Atmospheric height refers to the vertical distance between the surface and one of the standard
pressure charts used to analyze weather. This distance is measured in decameters (dam), where
1 dam = 10 meters.
Height is governed by the average temperature of the air column between the surface and the
given pressure value.
- Warm air expands, increasing the vertical distance between levels higher heights
- Cold air contracts, decreasing the vertical distance lower heights

Surface & Upper Air Station Plots
Wind Data: We observe and analyze the wind speed and direction by using wind barbs that are
attached to every station plot. A wind barb has two sections. First, a solid black line will be
connecting to a small, enclosed circle which represents the location of the weather station reporting
that data. The direction that line is pointing depicts the direction the wind is coming FROM based
on the cardinal directions previously discussed in this unit. Second, the tail end of the direction line
will tell you the wind speed. The speed will be depicted by either solid black lines, or triangles. A
triangle represents 50KT, a solid black line represents 10KT, and a black line that is half the length
of the 10KT line is 5KT.
EX. A station plot at 500mb with the direction line pointing directly West, with a black triangle, two
solid black lines, and a shorter black line would be wind coming from 270 degrees at 75KT.
Upper Air Plots: Station plots provide crucial data for weather analysis. The height of the
pressure level, typically displayed with three digits in decameters (tens of meters), is found in the
upper right of the station circle. Note that 300 and 500 mb charts are rounded to the nearest
decameter, while 700 and 850 mb charts are rounded to the nearest meter. The temperature in
degrees Celsius is located in the upper left of the circle; negative temperatures are indicated with
a minus sign (-). Finally, the dew point depression, also in degrees Celsius, is positioned in the
lower left.
EX. A station plot at 300mb with 924 to the upper right, -12 to the upper left, and 14 to the lower
left, would represent a station plot with a height of 924dam, a temperature of -12 degrees C and a dew-point
depression of 14 (meaning the dew point temperature would be -26 degrees C).
Surface Plots: The surface station plot model displays key information including wind direction
and speed (represented the same way as on upper-air charts), sea-level pressure, temperature, and
dew point temperature. Sea-level pressure is located on the upper right of the station circle,
represented by three digits to the nearest tenth of a millibar. The temperature, in either
Fahrenheit or Celsius, is found on the upper left, with a minus sign indicating negative values.
Finally, the dew point temperature is positioned on the lower left.
EX. A station plot at the surface with 013 to the upper right, 75 to the upper left, and 73 to the lower
left, would represent a station plot with a pressure of 1001.3mb, a temperature of 75 degrees F and a
dewpoint of 73 degrees F.

Lines of Equal Value
Using the data gathered from the surface and upper air plots, we can connect certain features that
have equal values based on the observations reported over a given location. There are lines on
each chart that represent equal values of a specific unit of measurement. The most common lines
of equal value are:

Isotherms: Isotherms are lines of equal temperature. By observing the temperature at different
weather stations, we can determine what the line is that separates colder temperatures from
warmer temperatures. Isotherms are depicted on charts as dashed red lines.
Isobars: Isobars are lines of equal pressure. Isobars are only found at the surface and are
measured in millibars represented by solid black lines. A closed isobar will represent either a
high- or low-pressure center which is the basis for all surface-based weather features you will
learn later in this unit.
Contours: Contours are very similar to isobars and are also depicted as solid black lines. Where
isobars measure lines of equal pressure at the surface, contours measure lines of equal height in either
meters or decameters. Just like isobars, a closed contour will represent either a high- or low-height
center.
Isotachs: Isotachs are lines of equal wind speed. By taking the wind data from our station plots,
we can determine the flow of equal wind speed. Isotachs are represented on a chart by dashed
purple lines.
Thickness: Thickness is the vertical distance between two constant pressure surfaces, typically
between 1,000 to 500 mb. It differs from contours because it includes a function of temperature
and moisture to compute the distance. Thickness lines are depicted as dashed red lines (540 may
be in blue). High thickness values indicate warm air; low thickness values indicate cold air.
Forces Affecting Parcel Movement
The atmosphere is always in motion due to differing atmospheric physics and forces. Air parcels
move horizontally and vertically in a fluid-like motion in a straight-line or curved wind pattern.
Gradient Force
A gradient describes the rate of change in value over distance. A weak gradient has a smaller
change in value over a certain distance, whereas a strong gradient has a greater change in value

over the same distance. Gradient force determines wind speed in the upper and lower
atmosphere. When gradient force increases, wind speeds also increase. When gradient force
weakens, wind speeds will decrease. There are two types of gradient force: Contour Gradient and
Pressure Gradient. Contour gradient force initiates winds at any level above the surface and
pressure gradient force initiates winds at the surface. Gradient Force is stronger around a low
height/pressure center and weaker with a high height/pressure center.
Coriolis Force (CoF)
The opposing force of gradient force is Coriolis Force. Coriolis Force is an apparent force that
deflects moving objects (like air flow) due to the Earth's rotation. In the northern hemisphere,
this deflection is to the right of the relative wind flow. Its strength varies with latitude; it's
strongest near the poles and weakest at the equator. The PGF and Coriolis force typically exist
in a state of balance, so as one increases or decreases, the other adjusts inversely to maintain that
equilibrium.

Frictional Force
Frictional force opposes parcel movement, and it only exists between the Earth's surface and the
boundary layer. Increasing terrain increases friction and decreasing terrain decreases friction. An
increase in friction results in a decrease in speed where a decrease in friction results in an
increase in speed.
Analyzing Atmospheric Flow
Straight Line vs. Curved Flow
When the Gradient and Coriolis force are equal and opposite, the air is in balance. This results in
straight-line flow without changing latitude.

When the Gradient force and Coriolis force are not equal, the atmosphere is in curved flow. This
imbalance causes air parcels to rotate and deflect. The direction of deflection (clockwise or
counterclockwise) depends on the relative strengths of the forces.
Cyclonic Circulation
Definition: Counterclockwise rotation of air parcels. Occurs when the Gradient force is stronger
than the Coriolis force, causing air to deflect to its left.
Troughing: Characterized by open cyclonic contours in the upper atmosphere, visually
represented as a solid black line indicating the axis of the trough.
Surface Low Pressure Center: Defined by closed isobars at the surface, with lower pressure
values at the center surrounded by higher pressure. Depicted on weather maps with a red "L".
Low Height Center: Defined by closed height contours aloft, with lower height values at the
center surrounded by higher heights. Depicted on weather maps with a red "L".
Anticyclonic Circulation

Definition: Clockwise rotation of air parcels. Occurs when the Gradient force is weaker than the
Coriolis force, causing air to deflect to its right.
Ridging: Characterized by open anticyclonic contours in the upper atmosphere, visually
represented as a jagged black line indicating the axis of the ridge.
Surface High Pressure Center: Defined by closed isobars at the surface, with higher pressure
values at the center surrounded by lower pressure. Depicted on weather maps with a blue "H".
High Height Center: Defined by closed height contours aloft, with higher height values at the
center surrounded by lower heights. Depicted on weather maps with a blue "H".

Atmospheric Flow
Dynamic Processes
Advection
Advection is a change in temperature or moisture due to horizontal movement of air over a
specific location.
Temperature Advection: Temperature advection is one of the most important causes of stability
change in the atmosphere. It is categorized as either warm air or cold air advection. Warm air
advection (WAA) signifies warmer temperatures moving into an area of relatively colder air
such as warm air from the equator moving toward the poles. The opposite exists with cold air
advection (CAA) which is colder air moving into an area of relatively warmer air such as when
cold air from the poles move toward the Equator. Warm air advection indicates decreasing
stability in the lower atmosphere, where cold air advection indicates increasing stability.
Temperature advection is most seen on analysis charts by a thermal gradient measured by the
distance between isotherms. Thermal troughing indicates colder air flowing toward the equator,
and thermal ridging indicates warm air flowing toward the poles.

Moisture Advection
Changes in dew-point temperature and dew-point depression signify dry or moist air advection.
Dry air advection occurs when dry, continental air moves into an area of moisture like the dry air
over land moving over a body of water. Moist air advection occurs when moist, maritime air
moves over a dry surface like air from a body of water moving onshore and over the land.

Barotropic vs. Baroclinic
Barotropic: In barotropic weather systems, temperature patterns (isotherms) closely match
pressure patterns (isobars), and height patterns (contours), indicating minimal temperature
advection. These systems are shallow, lacking influence from upper-level winds. Because they
are vertically aligned and not steered by upper-level features, barotropic systems tend to move
very slowly.

Baroclinic: In contrast to barotropic systems, baroclinic weather systems exhibit temperature
patterns (isotherms) that are more perpendicular to pressure patterns (isobars) and height patterns
(contours), indicating significant temperature advection. Surface features in these systems are
strongly linked to and supported by upper-level atmospheric features. Temperature changes

occur throughout the atmosphere, resulting in faster movement of the system in a downstream
direction.

Convergence, Divergence, and Vertical Motions
Vertical motions refer to the upward or downward movement of air parcels in the atmosphere,
driven by factors like temperature differences, pressure gradients, and convergence/divergence
patterns.

Convergence: Convergence occurs when winds collide, resulting in an accumulation of air mass.
This excess air is then forced to move vertically. Specifically, convergence at the surface
primarily results in upward vertical motion as warm air is forced aloft. Conversely, convergence
aloft typically forces cold air downwards, leading to downward vertical motion.
Divergence: Divergence happens when winds spread apart, creating a "void" or removal of air
mass. To fill this void, air from above and below must sink and rise, respectively. This means
warmer air from below will rise (upward vertical motion) and colder air from above will sink
(downward vertical motion) to compensate for the spreading air.
Chimney Effect: The "Chimney Effect" refers to the upward vertical motion of warm air caused
by either lower-level convergence, or upper-level divergence. The atmospheric effects of the
chimney effect results in surface pressure decreasing and upper-level heights to rise as the warm
air expands the atmosphere above it.
Damper Effect: The "Damper Effect" refers to the downward vertical motion of cold air caused
by either lower-level divergence, or upper-level convergence. The atmospheric effects of the
damper effect results in surface pressure rising and upper-level heights to fall as the cold air
compresses the atmosphere above it.
Level Of Non-Divergence (LND): Whether you analyze convergence or divergence at a point in
the atmosphere, the converging or diverging air parcels must switch to the opposite at some point
to maintain the chimney or damper effect. The Level of Non-Divergence refers to the area of the
atmosphere where the convergence or divergence switches to the opposing effect. The LND is
typically found at the halfway point in either chimney or damper effect as the parcels are either
rising or sinking. EX. If convergence occurs at the surface and extends up to 500mb ( 18000ft),
then 9000ft is where the convergence would start becoming divergence aloft.

and influence weather across entire continents and ocean basins. The two macroscale air masses

Continental Arctic (cA)
The Continental Arctic (cA) air mass forms over extremely cold land surfaces typically located
poleward of 60 degrees latitude in either hemisphere. These source regions include areas such as
Siberia, Greenland, and parts of Antarctica. This air mass reaches its greatest intensity and
spatial coverage during the winter season. the summer months, cA air weakens and retreats
toward the poles.
cA air masses are responsible for polar outbreaks, cold-core high pressure systems, and extreme
winter conditions. Their identification is crucial for understanding cold air advection and the
structure of polar air intrusions in both hemispheres.
Key Characteristics:
- Temperature: Extremely Cold
- Moisture: Very dry
- Stability: Extremely Stable
Chart Indicators:
- Thickness: Typically, < 528 dam on the thickness chart.
- Geography: Located over land masses.
Maritime Tropical (mT)
The Maritime Tropical (mT) air mass forms over warm tropical and subtropical oceans located
between the equator and approximately 35 degrees latitude in both hemispheres. Source regions include
the Caribbean Sea, western Pacific, Indian Ocean, and tropical South Atlantic. These regions
provide abundant heat and moisture, allowing the air mass to become warm, humid, and
unstable. mT air expands poleward during the summer season in each hemisphere, bringing hot,
moist conditions and supporting convective activity. In winter, it retreats equatorward and
weakens.
mT air masses are the primary source of moisture and instability for warm-season weather
systems across both hemispheres. They fuel thunderstorms, tropical cyclones, and frontal
boundaries, making them essential to identify when analyzing moisture transport and convective
potential.
Key Characteristics:
- Temperature: Hot Temperatures
- Moisture: Moist / Fully Saturated
- Stability: Extremely Unstable

Chart Indicators:
- Thickness: Typically, > 570 dam on the thickness chart.
- Geography: Located over large bodies of water.
Three Cell Model
Meteorologists use a three-cell model to describe how and where air moves within our global
circulation. The three cells are from the poles to equator in both hemispheres are the Polar,
Ferrel, and Hadley cells.
Polar Cell: The Polar Cell begins with a damper effect near the poles with convergence aloft and
divergence at the surface and extends equatorward with a chimney effect near 60 degrees latitude with
convergence at the surface and divergence aloft. The Polar Cell has the lowest tropopause out of
the three cells due to the cold polar air compressing the atmosphere to much lower heights than
the Ferrel or Hadley Cell usually near 300mb. The Polar Cell contains the continental arctic
airmass.
Ferrel Cell: The Ferrel cell begins with the chimney effect near 60 degrees latitude with convergence at
the surface and divergence aloft and extends equatorward with a damper effect near 30 degrees latitude
with divergence at the surface and convergence aloft. The Ferrel Cell has a tropopause height
somewhere between the Polar and Hadley Cell usually near 200mb.
Hadley Cell: The Hadley cell begins with a damper effect near 30 degrees latitude with divergence at
the surface and convergence aloft and extends equatorward with a chimney effect near the
equator with convergence at the surface and divergence aloft. The Hadley Cell has the highest
tropopause out of the three cells due to the warm tropical air near the Equator expanding the
atmosphere to much higher heights than the Polar or Ferrel Cell usually near 100mb. The Hadley
Cell contains the maritime tropical airmass.
Semi-Permanent Pressure Systems
With the interaction of the three cells that form our atmosphere, the resulting upward and
downward vertical motions create different barotropic pressure centers at the surface we refer to
as Semi-Permanent Pressure Systems. These systems fall into one of four categories.
Polar High: The damper effect near the poles creates a Continental Arctic (cA) air mass and
high-pressure center called the Polar High. This is a cold core system meaning cold air extends
throughout the atmosphere from the surface to the tropopause resulting in a low height center
above the surface high pressure center.
Subpolar Lows: The chimney effect near 60 degrees latitude creates low pressure centers at the surface
called subpolar lows. There are two in the northern hemisphere we call the Aleutian low and the
Icelandic low. Subpolar lows are not air masses but are still considered cold core system which
results in a low height center above the surface low pressure center. Subpolar Lows will migrate
with the seasons. They will move West in the Winter and East in the Summer.
Subtropical Highs: The damper effect near 30 degrees latitude creates a high-pressure center at the
surface called subtropical highs. The Northern hemisphere has two subtropical highs called the

North Pacific high and the Bermuda-Azores high. The Subtropical highs are maritime tropical
(mT) air masses, as well as warm-core systems resulting in a high height center above the
surface low pressure center. Subtropical Highs also migrate with the seasons. They move
opposite the Subpolar Lows. Subtropical Highs will move West in the Summer, and East in the
Winter.
Equatorial Lows: The chimney effect near the Equator creates equatorial low-pressure centers at
the surface. These are warm core systems that are more prevalent in the northern hemisphere
during the summer and the southern hemisphere during winter.

Jet Streams and the Long-Wave Pattern
Jet streams are strong horizontal winds concentrated in narrow bands located in the upper
atmosphere. They are thousands of miles long, hundreds of miles wide, and two to three miles
thick. There are two terms associated with jet streams:
Jet Stream: A band of wind speeds greater than or equal to 50 knots analyzed by isotachs.
Jet Axis or Jet Core: An interconnecting band of the highest wind speeds near the center of a jet
stream and is depicted on a chart by a solid red line.
Formation Requirements: Jet Streams are created by the interaction of the three cells as well as
strong horizontal temperature contrast. For example, there is relatively colder air on the poleward side
and relatively warmer air on the equatorward side of each jet stream. The temperature contrast is
measured perpendicular to the jet axis. If the temperature contrasts are relatively weak, it will be a weaker
jet stream. If the temperature contrast is greater, a stronger jet stream would form.
Three Tropospheric Jets
Arctic jet. The Arctic jet is associated with the Polar cell. It forms near the Arctic Circle and has
the lowest altitude of all three jets typically found near 400mb. Due to a weaker temperature
contrast in the cA air, the AJ is weaker than the PFJ.
Polar front jet (PFJ). The PFJ is located at the intersection of the Polar and Ferrel cells. It varies
in altitude and temperature more than the other two jets due to the largest temperature change
between seasons. The PFJ is found using the 300mb chart and is in large bands of wind speeds
of 50KT or more.
Subtropical jet (STJ). The STJ is located at the intersection of the Ferrel and Hadley cells. The
STJ has the highest altitude of all three jets, typically found near 150mb. Due to a weaker
temperature contrast in the mT air, the STJ is weaker than the PFJ.
Long Wave Pattern

The Long Wave Pattern is an upper air feature that is typically found between 25 degreesN and 65 degreesN
Latitude and describes the overall upper-level wind flow that is usually associated with the Polar
Front Jet. The Long Wave pattern is analyzed on a 500mb chart and can be found by finding the
strongest contour gradient between the 552 and 570 decameter contour lines.
Formation principals The Long Wave Pattern is made up of macroscale ridges and troughs that
flow west to east around the globe in the northern hemisphere:
- Long Wave Ridges: Poleward extensions of warm, maritime tropical (mT) air from
the equator and subtropical highs
- Long Wave Troughs: Equatorward extensions of cold, continental arctic (cA) air from
the poles and polar highs
The transition point between a trough and a ridge - where cyclonic flow begins turning into
anticyclonic flow - is called the Inflection Point.
Zonal vs. Meridional: There are two types of long-wave patterns: zonal and meridional. In a
zonal pattern, airflow is primarily flowing west to east, whereas a meridional pattern will have
more north to south flow. Regardless of the pattern, it separates cold, arctic air from warm,
tropical air in the upper atmosphere. On a planetary scale, seasonal changes and the interaction
of smaller scale weather features moving along the long-wave pattern are the reasons the long-
wave pattern will change between zonal and meridional flow. During summer in the northern

hemisphere, the long-wave pattern is primarily zonal due to warmer temperatures being able to
extend more poleward, increasing atmospheric heights. The pattern changes to meridional in the
other seasons due to a decrease in temperature allowing the cold, polar air to extend more
equatorward, decreasing atmospheric heights.

Introduction to Synoptic Scale Analysis
Weather features ranging in size from 650 miles to continental are considered synoptic scale
features. Synoptic scale analysis allows you to view multiple weather systems simultaneously on
one map. You can analyze systems individually as well as their relationships with other systems
surrounding them. Synoptic scale weather features are considered primary circulation patterns
and can be barotropic or baroclinic.

Synoptic Scale Air Masses
Synoptic-scale air masses are large regional bodies of air that influence weather across hundreds
to thousands of miles and persist for several days. These air masses are smaller and more
transient than macroscale air masses, but they play a critical role in shaping mid-latitude weather
systems. Synoptic-scale air masses include Maritime Polar (mP), Continental Polar (cP), and
Continental Tropical (cT). Each air mass has a distinct temperature and moisture characteristics
that affect cloud formation and precipitation.
Each air mass is identified using the same two letter code used to identify macroscale air masses.
- First letter: Moisture source
o c = Continental dry
o m = Maritime moist
- Second letter: Thermal origin
o P = Polar cold
o T = Tropical warm

Maritime Polar (mP)
The Maritime Polar (mP) air mass forms over large cold bodies of water in the mid-latitudes,
such as the North Pacific and North Atlantic Oceans. These regions provide cool temperatures
and abundant moisture, resulting in a damp, unstable air mass. mP air is most common in the
cooler seasons and frequently affects coastal regions.
Key Characteristics:
- Temperature: Cool / Cold
- Moisture: Moist / Saturated
- Stability: Moderately Unstable
Chart Indicators:
- Thickness: Between 528-540 dam on the thickness chart.
- Pressure: Will be depicted on a surface chart as a high-pressure center
- Why It Matters: mP air masses are essential for identifying marine-influenced weather
systems, especially in coastal zones. They contribute to cloud formation, fog
development, and precipitation in mid-latitude cyclones.
Continental Polar (cP)
The Continental Polar (cP) air mass originates over cold, dry continental interiors at mid to high
latitudes. Common source regions include Canada and central Eurasia. cP air is stable and dry,
especially in winter. In summer, cP air warms slightly but retains its dryness.
Key Characteristics:
- Temperature: Cold
- Moisture: Dry
- Stability: Stable
Chart Indicators:
- Thickness: Between 528-540 dam on the thickness chart.
- Pressure: Will be depicted on a surface chart as a high-pressure center
Why It Matters: cP air masses are crucial for identifying high-pressure systems. They help
define cold air advection and are often involved in the development of stable surface conditions.
Modified Polar (Mod mP/cP)
As mP/cP air moves equatorward away from its original source region, it begins absorbing heat
and becomes a modified polar airmass.
Key Characteristics:
- Temperature: Warming as the airmass moves equatorward
- Moisture: Increasing as the airmass moves equatorward.
- Stability: Stability decreasing as the airmass moves equatorward.
Chart Indicators:

- Thickness: Between 540-570 dam on the thickness chart.
Why It Matters: Mod cP/mP air masses are the equatorward extension of the same cP/mP
airmasses discussed previously. They will maintain the same characteristics, but the further they
move from their source region, the overall intensity of the airmass decreases.
Continental Tropical (cT)
The Continental Tropical (cT) air mass forms over hot, dry desert regions in subtropical
latitudes. Source regions include Southwestern United States, the Sahara Desert, and parts of
South Asia. cT air is most prominent during the warm season and is formed due to intense
surface heating and very low humidity. It is an unstable air mass, but due to the limited amount
of moisture, cloud and storm formation is rare.
Key Characteristics:
- Temperature: Very hot
- Moisture: Very dry
- Stability: Unstable
Chart Indicators:
- Thickness: Typically, > 570 dam on the thickness chart.
- Pressure: Will be depicted on a surface chart as a low-pressure center
Why It Matters: cT air masses are key players in heat waves and drought conditions. Their
identification is essential for forecasting thermal extremes.

Identifying Synoptic Scale Weather Features
Jet Maximum
A jet max is a small region of higher wind speeds that are surrounded by lower wind speeds. A
jet max is depicted on a 300mb chart as an area shaded purple along the polar front jet axis. As
this higher concentration of wind speed rotates along the PFJ axis, it pulls colder air toward the
surface and warmer air toward the tropopause at a greater intensity than the rest of the PFJ.
These temperatures being forcefully moved causes distortions in the Long-Wave pattern which
has a ripple from the upper levels down to the surface.

Major and Minor Short Waves
Both major and minor short waves are created by temperature distortions within the planetary
flow and are analyzed on charts by identifying ridging or troughing in the contours.
Major short waves are key upper-level weather features that help support surface low and high
pressure systems, especially those formed in areas with strong temperature contrasts (baroclinic
zones). These waves are typically analyzed on the 500 mb and 700 mb charts, where they
appear as large-scale ridges or troughs. Major short waves are baroclinic, meaning they are
driven by temperature differences, and they travel along the jet stream, staying close to the jet
axis as they move through the atmosphere.
Minor short waves are smaller scale disruptions to the atmospheric flow when compared to
major short waves. Minor short waves can exist on or off the jet axis and are too weak in
intensity to support surface pressure centers. If minor short waves intensity increases enough,
they can grow into a major-short wave. If a major-short waves intensity decreases enough, they
can shrink into a minor short-wave.

Major Short-Wave Trough (MSWT): Major short-wave troughs are identified by open cyclonic
circulation in the contours moving along a jet axis that are commonly found in areas of:
- Cyclonic turning contours, isotherms, and wind barbs
- Moisture downstream
- Upstream of larger areas of moisture
Meteorologically, they will have cold air, convergence, and downward vertical motion upstream
of its axis and warm air, divergence, and upward vertical motion downstream of its axis which
provides support for surface low-pressure centers.
Major Short-Wave Ridge (MSWR): Major short-wave ridges are identified by open anticyclonic
circulation in the contours moving along a jet axis that are commonly found in areas of:
- Anticyclonically turning contours, isotherms, and wind barbs
- Upstream of a jet max
Meteorologically, they will have warm air, divergence, and upward vertical motion upstream of
its axis and cold air, convergence, and downward vertical motion downstream of its axis which
provides support for surface high-pressure centers.

Cut-off High: Closed anticyclonic circulation in upper atmosphere analyzed at 500mb. These will
be located on the poleward side of the Polar Front jet axis. Formation process of a cut-off high
requires:
- The polar front jet transports warm air into the long-wave ridge upstream of the ridge
axis.
- The ridge continues to build and extends too far poleward which causes the jet to cut
across the contours, reforming the long wave ridge with a smaller amplitude.
- Warm air poleward of the jet creates a pocket of warm air surrounded by colder air.
- The cut-off warm air rises creating high height center
Cut-off highs are barotropic systems that NEVER extend to the Earth's surface due to the rising
warm air within the system only extending to higher heights.
Cut-off Low: Closed cyclonic circulation in upper atmosphere analyzed at 500mb. These will be
located on the equatorward side of the Polar Front jet axis. Formation process of a cut-off low
requires:
- The polar front jet transports cold air into the long-wave trough upstream of the trough
axis.
- The long-wave trough continues to deepen and extends too far equatorward which causes
the jet to cut across the contours, reforming the long wave trough with a smaller
amplitude.
- Cold air equatorward of the jet creates a pocket of cold air surrounded by warmer air.
- The cut-off cold air sinks creating low height center.
Cut-off lows are barotropic systems that may or may not reflect down to the surface due to
sinking cold air parcels adiabatically warming as they descend.

Barotropic High Pressure Center: Anticyclogenesis of barotropic high-pressure centers, also
called plateau highs, form on the Earth's surface due to intense surface cooling. They are shallow
domes of cold air with the strongest intensity and greatest expanse in the winter. Barotropic high-
pressure centers are cP air masses and form when cold air in the upper atmosphere sinks and gets
trapped in the terrain of mountain ranges. Cold air sinks between the terrain forming a high-
pressure center.
Barotropic Low Pressure Center: Intense surface heating creates barotropic low-pressure centers,
also called thermal lows. They are shallow domes of warm air primarily located over deserts.
Their intensity is strongest during summer and weakest in the winter. They are the only low-
pressure system that is also an air mass, specifically continental tropical (cT). The most common
places to find these cT lows are in North America between Mexico and Nevada the Sahara low
stretching east to west over the Saharan desert in Africa, and the Asiatic low extending east from
Saudi Arabia to Mongolia.

Baroclinic High Pressure Center: Baroclinic high-pressure centers have two air mass types,
continental polar (cP) and maritime polar (mP). Source regions for continental polar air masses
are poleward of 50 degreesN where the Polar High cA airmass resides. The lifecycle of a baroclinic
high-pressure center follows this process:
- The long-wave ridge extends poleward of 50 degreesN into the Arctic Circle (cA airmass).
- The upper-level convergent areas between the upstream side of a major short-wave
trough and the downstream side of a major short-wave ridge cause a strong damper effect
to form between 500 mb and the surface resulting in anticyclogenesis and the
development of a high-pressure center
- The baroclinic high center stacks dynamically back (upstream) to warm air from the
surface to 500mb. The entire system moves with the wind flow typically to the Southeast
toward the Maritime Tropical air.

- Once it leaves its source region, the polar air mass begins to modify and weaken due to
diabatic absorption of heat. However, if the MSWR remains upstream of the high-
pressure center, the cold air sinking into the system can keep it from completely
dissipating.
- Anticyclolysis occurs when the supporting major short-wave ridge outruns the surface
high-pressure resulting in a loss of cold air sinking to offset the diabatic effects.
- Eventually the system will weaken enough and get completely absorbed by the mT air.

Baroclinic Low Pressure Center (Wave Cyclones) & Fronts: Closed cyclonic circulation on the
Earth's surface surrounded by higher pressure. The four types of wave cyclones that a baroclinic
low will transition into throughout its lifecycle are unstable, mature, decaying, and stable waves.
A front describes the transition zone between two air masses of different densities and moisture
contents. A baroclinic high air mass interacting with the warm tropical air mass as it moves

equatorward result in a frontal boundary which causes cyclogenesis to occur at the interaction
due to the forced surface convergence.
Unstable wave
An unstable wave is a baroclinic low-pressure center with cold air upstream and warm air
downstream of the center. There are cold and warm fronts attached to the low-pressure center.
Cold front. Cold fronts have cold, northerly air behind the front and warm, southerly air ahead of
them. They are the leading edge of a polar air mass and are found in a cyclonic trough at the
surface extending from the surface low-pressure center. The depiction for cold fronts is a blue
line with triangular pips pointing in its direction of movement.
Cold fronts have two sections: Active Cold Front and Inactive Cold Front. Active cold fronts
extend southwest to west from an inactive cold front. They are associated with the anticyclonic
circulation around the high-pressure center. Inactive cold fronts extend south to southwest from
the low center. They are associated with the cyclonic circulation around the low center.
Warm front. Warm fronts have cold, northeasterly air ahead and warm southerly air behind the
front. It is the boundary between warm air east of the low center and the cold air that is to the
northeast of the low. The warm air moving poleward is forced aloft over the surface cold air. It
extends eastward from a low center through a surface trough. Its depiction is a red line with
semi-circle pips pointing in the front's direction of movement.
Cyclogenesis.
The divergence ahead of the major short-wave trough results in a chimney effect at the surface
above the low-pressure center. The surface low stacks back (upstream) to cold air aloft up to
500mb. The surface low centers move parallel to the PFJ axis toward the downstream long-wave
ridge.
Frontogenesis. Frontogenesis includes any atmospheric action or condition that increases the
thermal gradient associated with the surface frontal systems. There are three scenarios for
unstable waves:

1. Northerly wind speeds increase west of the low center pushing more cold air toward the
cold front. The thermal gradient increases on the poleward side of the front and cold
frontogenesis occurs.
2. Increasing wind speeds east of the low center pushes warm air toward the retreating cold
air mass ahead of the warm front. Warm air lifts above the surface at the frontal boundary
increasing the thermal gradient poleward of the front and warm frontogenesis occurs.
3. The unstable wave deepens due to increased divergence aloft. On the surface, cyclonic
circulation increases, the low-pressure field becomes larger, and warm and cold air
advection increases around the low center. The overall result is cyclogenesis creates
warm and cold frontogenesis.
If the unstable wave continues to receive upper-level support from the MSWT, it will deepen
into a mature wave.

Mature wave
A mature wave is a baroclinic system with low-height centers that has deepened enough to begin
reflecting in the upper atmosphere.
Cyclogenesis. Mature waves develop from unstable waves. A thermal ridge of warm air
develops north-northwest of the surface low-pressure center as warm air continues to rotate
cyclonically around the pressure center. As the low attempts to maintain the warm air advection
downstream of its center, it will begin to separate itself from the jet axis onto the poleward side
of the PFJ. The wave continues to deepen with cold air advection west and warm air advection
east of the surface and 850-mb low centers. Cyclonic rotation increases at lower levels, and with
the low moving off the jet axis, an occluded front forms at the triple point along the jet axis
attempting to keep the low center connected to the jet axis and the warm and cold fronts.
Occluded front. Occluded fronts are a combination of cold and warm fronts that are attempting
to keep the low that is moving away from the triple point connected to the jet axis/frontal system.
Their depiction is a purple line with triangular and semi-circle pips next to each other pointing in
the same direction. Occluded fronts extend poleward from the triple point through the 850mb
thermal ridge and connect with the low that is now on the poleward side of the jet axis.
Stationary Front
A stationary front develops with a mature wave extending westward from the active cold front. A
stationary front is depicted on surface charts by alternating red and blue pips extending west
from the active cold front and ending southwest of the high-pressure center where the
anticyclonic flow turns back poleward. Equatorward of the stationary front is warmer air and
westerly wind flow. Poleward of the stationary front is colder air and easterly wind flow.
System movement and Modification: The surface low center will continue to deepen and move
poleward as long as the 500mb MSWT remains upstream and provides the upper-level support.
Cold and warm fronts remain attached to the triple point and progress downstream along the jet
axis. In the late stage, the divergence aloft decreases as the major short-wave trough begins to

outrun the surface low-pressure center. At this point, the system loses upper-level support,
becomes vertically stacked and transitions to a decaying wave.
Decaying wave
system from the surface to 500mb becomes barotropic and significantly slows. Divergence aloft
is gradually replaced by convergence, and the system begins to fill from the surface up to 500mb.
Stable wave
Stable waves are low-pressure centers created by differential heating along the stationary frontal
boundary of a mature or decaying wave. They are short-lived since they are typically a daytime
feature. Cyclolysis typically occurs with night-time cooling. Cyclogenesis may occur if a MSWT
approaches the stable wave and provides upper the divergence aloft the low-pressure needs to
develop further. If this happens, the stable wave will transition to a new unstable wave, and the
process of the wave cyclone will begin again.

Thunderstorm Atmospheric Ingredients
Many of the most severe weather features will originate from strong thunderstorms. For a
thunderstorm to develop, there are certain atmospheric dynamics that are required to produce
and maintain a thunderstorm. The main "ingredients" that are needed are (MILE):
(M)Moisture
All thunderstorms require moisture. Moisture sources include large bodies of water such as the
oceans or large lakes. You learned previously how to analyze moisture and moist air advection.
You will need to analyze the synoptic level flow influencing the area. If the overall flow
upstream of your location is coming from a moisture source, then you have the first ingredient
needed for thunderstorm development.
(I)Instability
The next ingredient needed is an unstable atmosphere. For thunderstorms to develop, you need a
favorable environment that enhances upward vertical motion. The more unstable the
atmosphere, the easier air parcels rise. Inversely, the more stable the atmosphere, the more
upward vertical motion is restricted, and the air parcels will not be able to rise to the height
required for thunderstorms to develop.
Cold & Warm Air Advection: One of the causes of an unstable atmosphere is cold air over warm
air. If you have an increase in cold air advection aloft, or warm air advection at the surface, you
would be increasing the temperature contrast between the upper and lower atmosphere, which
would cause the atmosphere to become more unstable.
Daytime Heating & Nighttime Cooling: Daytime heating can also cause more instability due to
the surface temperatures rising which would increase the temperature contrast between the
surface air and the now relatively colder air above it. The opposite would also be true, that
nighttime cooling would increase the cold air near the surface and would cause stability to
increase.

(L)Lift
If air parcels are rising, then a lifting mechanism is present. Assuming you have sufficient
moisture and an unstable atmosphere, there must be something to lift the parcels off the surface
and will be enhanced by an unstable environment. In weather, there are two categories of lifting
mechanism that affect rising air parcels:
Free Convection: Free convection describes air parcels being lifted from the surface due to
daytime heating. As the surface temperature increases, the air parcel is lifted from the surface
and begin cooling adiabatically. It is called free convection because there are no weather
features affecting the upward vertical motion of the air parcel.
Forced Convection: Forced convection describes air parcels that are lifted into the atmosphere
due to the influence of surrounding weather features. Forced convection occurs when something
forces convergence at the surface like a frontal boundary, or something forcing divergence aloft
like a Major Short-Wave Trough.
The strength of the lifting mechanism and the amount of instability are closely related, and one
can impact the other. The more unstable the environment, the less lift you need. Alternately, the
more stable your environment, the stronger your lift needs to be to develop thunderstorms.
(E)Exhaust
The last ingredient needed to maintain and strengthen a thunderstorm is exhaust. With
instability and lift, all that energy and upward vertical motion must go somewhere. If there is
nothing providing exhaust within a storm, a thunderstorm may develop, but it will eventually
collapse in on itself.
If the thunderstorm is to continue increasing in size and intensity, there must be a form of
exhaust in the upper levels to allow the excess rising energy somewhere to go to maintain the
constant flow of upward vertical motion. We can analyze exhaust by looking for areas of
divergence or strong wind bands of 40KT or more at 500mb near the developing thunderstorm.

Thunderstorm Types and Intensities
Thunderstorm Types
Single Cell: Single cell thunderstorms, also called air mass thunderstorms, are formed due to free
convection. They are short-lived storms usually lasting 30 to 60 minutes and potential severe
conditions include high wind speeds and small to medium-sized hail.
Multicellular
There are two types of multicellular thunderstorms: Mesoscale Convective Complexes (MCC)
and Mesoscale Convective Systems (MCS). The average duration for both types is 6 to 12 hours,
and they form in regions of strong directional shear in the lower levels with strong speed shear
aloft. These types of thunderstorms can cover a large area and are typically made up of several
individual storm cells. Individual cell motions move with the average 700mb wind direction

which tilts the storm as well as increases instability and the strength of the thunderstorms upward
vertical motion we call the updraft. Potential severe conditions include possible flash floods, large
hail, and short-lived tornadoes. Both large hail and tornadoes associated with multi-cell storms
occur most often in the early afternoon with the first thunderstorms due to the aid of daytime
heating.
Supercell
Supercells can be frontal or orographic. Orographic storms have an average duration of 1 to 3
hours and are associated with forced convection on the upstream side of mountain ranges.
Frontal storms have an average duration of 4 to 8 hours and are commonly found on the warm
side of the cold front. Like Multicell thunderstorms, a Supercell is made up of a very large storm
cell that has been able to develop and increase intensity due to an abundance of each ingredient
discussed earlier. Supercell thunderstorms are the most likely to produce the most severe weather
conditions such as large hail, high damaging winds, and tornadoes.
Thunderstorm Intensities
There are three intensities or types of thunderstorms: standard, moderate, and severe.
Standard: Standard thunderstorms have present lightning within 5 miles of a location, with or
without rain, surface winds less than 35KT, and no hail.
Moderate: Moderate thunderstorms contain the same thresholds as standard thunderstorms but
have surface winds of 35-49KT, and/or hail less than 3/4 inch.
Severe: If surface winds reach 50KT or higher, or hail is present with a diameter of 3/4 inch or
greater, the thunderstorm's intensity is severe. Tornadic thunderstorms most often occur with
moderate to severe thunderstorms.

Identifying Mesoscale Weather Features
Hail
Hail forms in the updraft (area of upward vertical motion enhancing lift) of a thunderstorm near
the freezing level (the height of 0oC above the surface). Hail size will vary based upon the
strength of the updraft. A strong updraft produces large hail while a weak updraft produces small

hail. Thunderstorm updrafts lift raindrops to freezing temperatures where internal thunderstorm
winds push the raindrops between warmer and colder temperatures. The more these raindrops
travel between above and below freezing altitudes, layers form on the hailstone causing them to
increase in size. When the hailstones become too heavy for the updraft to keep aloft, they fall to
the surface. Although possible with any type of thunderstorm, hail is most common with
supercell thunderstorms and is usually associated with other severe weather features.
Squall Line
A squall line is a line of thunderstorms downstream of an inactive cold front. Their formation
begins when 850-mb winds are faster than the surface winds behind the cold front. The upper
winds push cold air downstream of the surface front creating a convergent zone on the surface
with the southerly winds southeast of a low-pressure center creating a line of thunderstorms 50-
150 miles ahead of the inactive cold front.
Tornadoes
Tornadoes are violently rotating columns of air that extend from a thunderstorm to the surface.
Tornadoes can be extremely destructive and appear most commonly with severe thunderstorms.
Ideal conditions involve mixing warm, moist air, cool, dry air, and enough wind shear (changing
wind speed / direction) with height to create rotation within a thunderstorm's updraft. This rotation
is then tilted vertically within the storm and eventually reaching the surface.
Tornado Types
- Rope - These are very thin and have a rope-like appearance.
- Cone - Shape is narrower on ground and wider at the cloud base. It is generally more
dangerous than a rope tornado.
- Stovepipe - If the base and top have the same width it is a stovepipe tornado
- Wedge - The largest and most destructive tornado type. They are wider than they are tall
with a width of 1/2 mile or greater.
Stages of Development:
- Initial - Majority of the tornado is invisible due to a lack of debris.
- Intensification - Debris extends from the surface to the cloud base. A condensation
funnel cloud may be present.
- Mature - Tornado attains maximum width and it is very intense. Surface damage results
from directional and speed shear.
- Dissipating - Tornado decreases diameter, tilts away from vertical, and its damage path
decreases.
- Decaying - Tornado resembles a rope-like tube as the vortex lifts from the surface.
Intensities. We measure tornado intensity using the Enhanced Fujita Scale. It estimates tornado
speeds based on a variety of damage indicators and ranging from visible damage to complete
destruction. The Fujita scale has six levels ranging from EF0-EF5.
Thunderstorm Downburst
Thunderstorm downbursts typically occur in areas with higher levels of instability fueled by
high surface temperatures, as well as the presence of an extremely dry layer of air in the mid-

levels around 700mb. This dry air increases cooling of falling precipitation, increasing its
density, resulting in air rushing to the ground faster than the falling precipitation. Depending on
the levels of instability and the intensity of the dry air intrusion, wind speeds from a downburst
may be anywhere from 35KT to exceeding 100KT.

Freezing Precipitation
Freezing precipitation forms in areas where the upper levels are below freezing and falling
precipitation passes through a warm layer (temperatures above freezing), then re-enter a cold
layer (temperatures below freezing) just before hitting a surface that is also at or below freezing.
Freezing precipitation occurs when the layer of freezing air close to the surface is shallow
enough that the precipitation does not have enough time to completely freeze before reaching
the surface. Instead, the water freezes on contact with the surface, creating a coating of ice on
the surface.

Freezing precipitation is one of the most hazardous weather features and occurs most often
poleward of surface warm fronts. The surface ahead of (poleward) of a warm front could be
below freezing, while the rising warm air in a slope ahead of the front creates the warm layer
aloft needed for freezing precipitation to occur.

Upslope and Downslope Winds
Upslope winds form when the surface synoptic flow is perpendicular to a mountain range.
Surface winds move up the mountain increasing orographic lift causing clouds and potential
precipitation or thunderstorms to develop on the windward (upstream) side of the mountain.
Downslope winds require stable air near a mountaintop with the upper-level synoptic flow
perpendicular to the mountain range. Upper-level winds push cold air down the lee side

(downstream) of the mountain where the warm air toward the base of the mountain is forced
upward increasing upward vertical motion.
Sea Breeze and Land Breeze
Sea Breeze: A sea breeze forms along continental coastlines when surface winds blow from
water to land. It is a daytime feature that reaches its peak in the afternoon. Land heats quicker
than water during the day resulting in warmer air over the land and cooler air over the water.
The warm air rises and cooler air from coastal waters replaces the warm air. Moist, onshore
flow converges over land producing potential air mass thunderstorms in a line parallel to the
coast 5-10 miles inland.
Land Breeze: Land breeze occurs when surface winds blow from the land to water. It is a
nighttime feature reaching its peak in the early morning before sunrise. During the night, water
retains heat longer than land leaving warmer air over the water. The warm air rises and cooler air
over the land replaces the warm air. Offshore flow converges over water producing clouds and
potential air mass thunderstorms over the water near the shoreline.

Mist and Fog
Mist and fog form when air near the surface becomes saturated, reducing visibility due to
suspended water droplets. They are typically early morning features, often developing overnight
and dissipating after sunrise. Fog typically occurs when air parcels become fully saturated due
to recent precipitation, moist air advection, or upslope winds. Mist forms under similar
conditions but with less restriction to visibility. Mist and fog are common in areas under high
atmospheric stability, with winds less than 10 knots, and clear skies.

Detection Process
Satellite imagery can detect clouds and weather features between weather reporting stations.
Even though all satellite systems view the Earth from orbit they do so in different manners with
differing coverage and resolutions. Satellite sensors scan the Earth and atmosphere for
electromagnetic radiation (EMR). This includes visible, infrared and microwave radiation.
Systems onboard the satellite digitize this EMR data to radio waves and then transmit the radio
waves to forecast centers to be processed into different satellite imagery.
Imagery Types
Visible (VIS) Imagery
Visible imagery senses reflected, visible radiation. It is only available during the daytime
because it detects the illumination (brightness) of objects. The amount of illumination is
dependent upon four factors: the time of day, time of year, latitude, and cloud heights.
Infrared (IR) Imagery
Infrared sensors detect emitted short and long-wave infrared radiation. IR imagery is available 24
hours a day and includes the temperature of Earth's surface, which varies diurnally. During
daylight hours land is typically warmer than water, so land will appear darker. Conversely,
during nighttime hours water is typically warmer than land, so water will appear darker.
Water Vapor (WV) Imagery
Water vapor imagery measures mid-range infrared radiation. Cold temperatures will appear
black, while warm temperatures will appear white. Black and darker gray shades depict dry air.
Conversely, white and lighter gray shades are assumed to be moist air. Because WV imagery
falls on the infrared spectrum, it is available 24 hours a day.
Interpretation Considerations
When viewing satellite imagery, there are several factors that have an influence on the
interpretation of the product. Different imagery types are best suited to identify different weather
features and conditions. IR imagery is best utilized to identify high clouds due to their colder
temperatures. Visible imagery works best when identifying low clouds due to the water droplets
in low clouds having higher albedo than the ice crystals in high clouds. WV imagery best detects

middle and upper-level moisture making it useful for identifying the jet stream and areas of
upper-level circulation, especially in areas without clouds.

Surface Features
These features do not move during image animation and are most evident under clear skies.

Atmospheric Features
Dust and Sand. Areas of dust and sand appear in various shapes and sizes and will have a sharp
upstream edge (source region) and a diffuse downstream edge. In visible imagery, they appear

That is because there is little to no temperature contrast.
Smoke and Volcanic Ash. Smoke and volcanic ash is plume-shaped (thin line that broadens out

Ship Trails. Anomalous cloud lines may be ship trails formed from a ship's exhaust. They occur
most often in persistent maritime anticyclones and may last for days. They are bright white on
VIS imagery and virtually invisible on IR due to little or no temperature contrast. If ship trails
are on IR, they have a medium gray color.
Contrails. Contrails are small, white cloud lines formed from an aircraft's exhaust. On VIS
imagery they may cast a shadow on lower clouds while they are virtually invisible without an
enhancement in IR imagery.

Cloud Features
A cloud is a visible accumulation of minute water droplets and/or ice particles suspended in the
atmosphere above the Earth's surface. We separate clouds by the height of the cloud's base using
three etages: low, middle, and high.

Low etage - clouds lower than 7,000 feet
Cumulus (CU): Cumulus clouds are small to medium sized with slight vertical extent. They are
popcorn-shaped and generally have a bright white appearance.
On VIS imagery, cumulus clouds are small white circular elements in an unorganized cloud
field. They generally have a uniform appearance.
Towering cumulus (TCU): Towering cumulus clouds are medium to large with a moderate vertical
extent. They resemble columns or tower that are widely spaced apart and their tops and sides are
bright white with gray bases.

Towering cumulus also appears as white circular cloud shapes on VIS imagery. The cloud
element is like cumulus, but larger. On IR imagery towering cumulus appears lighter gray to
white.
Cumulonimbus (CB): Cumulonimbus clouds are large to huge with great vertical extent. Their tops
vary in shape; they can be cauliflower or anvil shaped. The tops and sides are bright white with very
dark gray to black bases. In some cases, cumulonimbus clouds have pouches hanging underneath
the cloud's base. When that occurs, its name changes to Cumulonimbus Mammatus. This is the
easiest cloud element to identify.
It has a large circular shape (bigger than towering cumulus), and it is bright white on VIS
imagery. It may have an anvil plume with a sharp upstream edge that becomes thin and diffuse
downstream. It may also have an overshooting top with small bulges or protrusions centered
above the anvil. Cumulonimbus often casts shadows on lower clouds making it easier to detect
the tallest cloud tops. On IR imagery, cumulonimbus clouds appear bright white without any
enhancement and could have small areas of color indicating the location of overshooting tops.
Fog / Stratus (FG / ST): Fog / Stratus clouds are wide with diffuse edges, and they form in a
uniform sheet with no vertical extent. They appear light gray or medium gray color.
Fog and stratus appear the best on VIS imagery. On VIS imagery, fog and stratus appear as a
white, unform sheet. They have sharp outlines without any texture or lumps There are some
differences between the two. For example, fog conforms to terrain features. It also shrinks or

dissipates from the outside edges towards the center. Stratus may move while fog will generally
remain stationary. It is best to supplement satellite images with surface observations when it is
difficult to tell the difference between them.
Middle etage - clouds between 7,000 feet and 18,000 feet
Altocumulus (AC): Altocumulus clouds resemble stratocumulus clouds, but the individual elements
are much smaller. They are widely spaced and circular in shape with little to no vertical extent and
are "dirty" white to light gray.
On VIS imagery altocumulus is a white, non-uniform cloud shield. It has a lumpy texture and
individual cloud elements are hard to see. It looks like stratocumulus, but the texture is more
visible, and altocumulus is brighter.

Altostratus (AS): Altostratus clouds form in a continuous, uniform sheet with diffuse edges and they
normally cover the entire celestial dome. They have no vertical extent, and their appearance is light
gray.
Altostratus and nimbostratus both appear light gray to white on VIS imagery and have a uniform
cloud field with little to no texture.
Nimbostratus (NS): Nimbostratus is a thick, dark gray cloud with little to moderate vertical extent.
The vertical extent can be misleading since it is different from the other clouds. Nimbostratus forms
in the middle etage, but as it develops the cloud's base lowers towards the Earth's surface.
Oftentimes it lowers into the low etage.

Nimbostratus appear lighter gray to white on VIS imagery. They have a uniform cloud field with
little to no texture.
High etage - clouds higher than 18,000 feet.
Cirrus (CI): Cirrus clouds are thin and wispy forming in hooks or filaments. They have no vertical
extent and are bright white.
Cirrus has a milky white appearance on VIS imagery. Thin cirrus is usually invisible due to
contamination while dense cirrus may cast shadows on lower clouds. Regardless, cirrus forms in
streaks or bands. It is bright white on IR imagery. Both thin and dense cirrus are noticeable.

Hemispheric Features
Baroclinic Leaf. Baroclinic leaves are light gray to white on VIS and IR imagery. The bright colors
are typically a cirrostratus cloud shield that hides any other cloud elements. The baroclinic leaf
normally has a shallow S-shape that may or may not have a V-notch in the trailing edge. The
baroclinic leaf typically forms near the base of a long-wave trough.

A comma cloud is light gray to bright white on both VIS and IR imagery. Comma clouds will
always be found downstream of the long-wave trough axis. There are several parts of the comma
cloud.
Surge Region: This is the upstream edge of the comma cloud. The surge region is the point
where the fastest winds intersect with the comma cloud, typically making it a narrow cloud-free
line.
Comma Tail: The comma tail is a long narrow band of cumulonimbus and towering cumulus
clouds extending equatorward. The upstream edge is the vorticity comma cloud, and the
downstream cirrus shield is the baroclinic zone.
Cusp: The cusp is the upstream edge of the comma cloud. It typically has a cyclonically oriented
circular shape.

Baroclinic Zone: The baroclinic zone is in the northeast quadrant of the comma cloud. Best
viewed on VIS imagery, it is the area of the bright white cirrostratus before the clouds begin to
separate and become more diffuse and fragmented along the leading edge of the comma cloud.
Deformation Zone: The Deformation Zone is the extreme poleward side of the entire comma
cloud.
Long-wave trough

The upstream side of a long-wave trough is generally cloud free. Cyclonically orientated, cirrus
streaks form parallel to the wind flow outlining a long-wave trough. Analyze the trough axis in
the center of the greatest cyclonic turning. Other indicators include a baroclinic leaf and comma
cloud. The baroclinic leaf forms near the base of a long-wave trough and comma clouds are
always downstream of the long-wave trough axis.
Long-wave ridge
The downstream side of a long-wave ridge is typically cloud free. Anticyclonically orientated,
cirrus streaks form parallel to the wind flow outlining a long-wave ridge. Analyze the ridge axis
in the center of the greatest anticyclonic turning. Additionally, comma clouds are always
upstream of a long-wave ridge axis.
Polar Front Jet
When analyzing for the Polar Front Jet (PFJ) on satellite imagery place the jet axis in the center
of a baroclinic leaf's V-notch or nearly center mass of a baroclinic leaf with a well-defined S-
shape. It also applies to the center of the U-shape associated with the surge region of a comma
cloud. The Polar Front Jet is easiest to identify using Water Vapor imagery by observing the
boundary between the dark (dry) and bright (moist) regions in the middle and upper troposphere.

Synoptic Scale Features
Major short-wave troughs
The areas where satellite imagery can help locate major short-wave troughs primarily due to the
divergence and upward vertical motion downstream of the trough axis. The first is on the
upstream side of TCU or CB clouds. The S-shape of a baroclinic leaf is the second instance. The
trough axis is slightly upstream of the cloud mass center.
Major short-wave ridges
Short-wave ridges are normally cloud free. Satellite imagery may have anticyclonically oriented
cloud elements. A major short-wave ridge may be present there, but it is always best to verify it
with another product. Water vapor imagery or animated imagery are best for verifying the
location of major short-wave ridges.
Low height centers
In a comma cloud, the low center is located at the cusp. With a cut-off low the image's
appearance varies; it can be nearly cloud free or it can have a comma shape. Either way, there
will be noticeable cyclonic rotation in the cloud field.

High height centers
High height centers are normally cloud free. There may be a C-shape that appears on the western
edge of the high center. The best image for identifying high height centers and the C-shape is
water vapor imagery.

Low Pressure Center
Satellite images display non-occluded and occluded low-pressure systems. They have large
cloud masses that typically obscure the low center.
Non-occluded system
A baroclinic leaf represents a non-occluded low-pressure system also known as an unstable
wave. The low center is slightly downstream of the baroclinic leaf's center between the cloud's
S-inflection point and its easternmost (leading) edge. A cold front is on the equatorward side of
the cloud mass extending from the low center to the trailing edge of the S-shape. A warm front
is also on the equatorward side of the cloud mass extending from the low center to the leading
edge of the S-shape.
Occluded system
Cold fronts transition through the comma tail extending from the triple point to the end of the
comma tail. The inactive cold front begins at the triple point behind the comma tail. It changes
to an active cold front where the comma tail bends the most. The active front then extends to the
front of the comma tail before ending at the end of the comma cloud.

The baroclinic zone often obscures the warm front. The front extends downstream from the
triple point to the farthest eastern portion of the cloud shield. If present, the warm front ends at a
V-notch or wedge in the leading edge of the baroclinic zone.
Comma clouds are associated with occluded systems whether it is a mature or decaying wave.
The low-pressure center location is between the surge region and the cusp for both systems. For
mature waves, it is closer to the surge region since the system is still baroclinic. Decaying waves
are barotropic, so the low center is closer to the cusp.
The occluded front extends upstream from the triple point along the bottom edge of the comma
head. Stationary fronts are the most difficult to locate. They are on the equatorward side of a
fragmented comma tail. The front extends upstream from an active cold front and may continue
along the center of a rope cloud if present. Rope clouds are narrow lines of cumulus or towering
cumulus clouds that form over water.

High pressure centers
The downward vertical motion associated with a high-pressure center dissipates any clouds
making it difficult to locate the center. However, if clouds are present, you will most likely see
Cumulus, Towering Cumulus, and Cumulonimbus clouds southwest of the high, and fog or
stratus to the southeast of the high. Poleward of the high will usually be clear or have thin mid to
high level clouds.

Introduction to Surface Weather Observations
Surface weather observations are coded data used to forecast weather conditions and issue
weather warnings worldwide. Observations are recorded hourly. During periods of adverse
weather conditions, they may be reported more frequently as needed. Observations are extremely
important to military operations. They directly affect aviation safety, provide commanders vital

Type of Observations
For meteorological observations the 'point of observation' is defined as the designated spot
where the elements of an observation are viewed and/or sensed. The point of observation is
within 5 statute miles of the airfield and affords as clear a view as possible of the runway
complex.
METAR
METARs contain a complete report of wind, visibility, present weather, sky condition,
temperature, dew point and altimeter setting collectively referred to as "the body of the
observation." In addition, encoded and/or plain language information that elaborates on data in
the body of the observation is appended at the end of the METAR in the remarks (RMK) section.

SPECI
Selected Special Weather Report (SPECI). A SPECI is an unscheduled observation completed
and transmitted when any of the special criteria listed in the observation manuals are observed or
sensed. SPECIs contain all data elements found in a METAR plus additional remarks that
elaborate on data in the body of the observation. All SPECI reports will be prepared and
transmitted as soon as possible after the relevant criteria are observed. The time ascribed to a
SPECI reflects the time, to the nearest minute, that the SPECI criteria are first met or observed.
Automated Observations
An automated observation describes a type of observation that is solely based on the data
gathered from the airfield sensor. The data is gathered at a set time and encoded into an
observation based on only what the sensor displays. There is no input from the observer and no
manipulation of the data it sends. This is usually the type of observation that is sent while the
airfield is closed and there is not an observer present to augment the sensor data before
submitting the observation.
Augmented Observations
Augmented observations are like automated observations in that they begin by pulling sensed
data and formatting the observation, but it does not automatically submit the observation.
Augmentation refers to an observer having the ability to go outside to the observation point and
validate what the sensed data produced. Depending on what the observer sees, they may add,
remove, or change the data given by the sensor before submitting the observation.
Manual Observations
Manual observations are exactly what the name implies. This type of observation requires the
observer to go to the observation point and manually gather the required data, encode the data,
and submit the observation without any input from the airfield sensor. Most elements of an

observation can be gathered just by an observer using their senses to see, feel, and hear certain
elements. However, for the elements of an observation not able to be assessed accurately by the
human eye, there are handheld instruments available to an observer to gather the data of those
elements. The most common instrument used to obtain this data is the Kestrel.

Station Identifier
The International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) designates all station identifiers using four
alphanumeric characters. The first letter represents the airfield's aeronautical fixed service
routing area. The remaining three characters specify the observation site. For example, KBIX is
the station identifier for Keesler AFB, Mississippi.
Date/Time of Observation
This is the date and time of the observation in Coordinated Universal Time (UTC), which is the

all observation sites, and it is referred to as "Zulu" (Z). The day of the month is the first two
digits, and the remaining four numbers is the observation in hours (second two numbers) and
minutes (last two numbers). The last character (Z) represents the standard, UTC time.

Prevailing Visibility
Visibility is a measure of the opacity of the atmosphere and is expressed in terms of the
horizontal distance at which a person can see and identify specified objects. Visibility values are
reported in statute miles for CONUS and meters for OCONUS. A visibility binder is a collection
of labeled photographs, lists or other means of identifying objects with known distances to be
used as aids when manually determining visibility ranges from the primary observation point.
Runway Visual Range (RVR)
RVR is an instrumentally derived value that represents the horizontal distance that a pilot can see
down the runway. The maximum distance in the direction of takeoff or landing at which the
runway, or specified lights or markers delineating it, can be seen from a position above a
specified point on its center. For all locations reporting RVR, the unit of measure will correspond
to the units of measure used to report visibility.

Present weather refers to any weather element occurring within 5 SM of the point of observation.

is restricted, present weather may include one weather group or multiple weather groups found in

Sky Condition
Sky condition is the amount of clouds and obscuring phenomena covering the celestial dome. The
celestial dome is the amount of sky not including any obstructions such as trees, mountains or
human-made structures.
Layer Amount: The celestial dome is split into eights or "octas". The standard format for sky
condition includes a contraction for the layer amount, layer height, and significant cloud type.
Each layer requires its own group so the observation may include one or more than one group.
Sky condition is encoded in ascending order up to the first overcast layer using the summation
principle. Layer amount refers to the number of octas a certain layer of clouds cover. Layer
amounts are clear (CLR or SKC / no clouds present), few (FEW 1/8 - 2/8 coverage), scattered
(SCT 4/8 - 5/8 coverage), broken (BKN 5/8 - less than total coverage), and overcast (OVC /
total sky coverage).
Decoding Sky Condition: When decoding sky condition from a METAR or SPECI report, the
layer height refers to the altitude in feet above ground level (AGL) where the base of a cloud
layer begins. It is encoded in hundreds of feet using a three-digit format:
- 001 = 100 feet
- 010 = 1,000 feet
- 100 = 10,000 feet
This format allows observers and forecasters to quickly interpret cloud base heights from
encoded data.
When encoding sky condition into a METAR or SPECI, cloud base heights must be rounded
down to the nearest reportable increment based on altitude range. This ensures consistency and
standardization across all observations.
Altitude Range (AGL) Reportable Increment Example
- 0-6,000 ft Nearest 100 ft 2,475 ft 2,400 ft 024
- 6,001-10,000 ft Nearest 500 ft 6,750 ft 6,500 ft 065
- Above 10,000 ft Nearest 1,000 ft 11,800 ft 11,000 ft 110
Always round down, never up-even if the measured value is close to the next increment.
Layer Height: Layer height indicates at what height in feet the base of each cloud layer starts.
Layer heights are in hundreds of feet at ground level (AGL) in a three-digit format. For
example, a code of 001 equals 100 feet, a code of 010 is 1,000 feet, and 100 equals 10,000 feet.

significant cloud types so it may or may not be included in the observation. Some examples of

- FEW080 - Few coverage with bases at 8,000 feet
- SCT025CB - Scattered coverage with bases at 2,500 feet with a cumulonimbus cloud
observed.
- FEW007 BKN025 OVC110 - Few coverage with bases at 700 feet, broken coverage
beginning at 2,500 feet, and overcast at 11,000 feet
There are two coverages for a clear sky. The difference between them is an automated observing
station reports CLR when there are no clouds between the sensor and 12,000 feet while a manual
observing station reports SKC when there are no clouds in the celestial dome.

Decoding PIREPS
Each PIREP must contain:
- Message Type
- Location
- Time
- Flight Level
- Type of Aircraft
- At least one other element to include either sky cover, present weather, temperature, flight
level, wind direction and speed, turbulence, or icing.

Station Identifier
The airfield identifier of the unit transmitting the pilot's observation. This identifier will be the
same as the identifier used in that station's observations.
Message Type
There are two message types: UA (routine report) or UUA (urgent) report.
Location
The point (/OV) where a pilot observed weather phenomena. It has different formats. For single
location reports, the location can be an airport identifier to include during climb (DURC) away
from the airfield or during descent (DURD) to the airfield. It can also be a latitude/longitude
coordinate such as 2139N 06525W. Pilots can also report weather between two locations using
airfield identifiers, latitude/longitude coordinates, or a combination of both.
Time
The UTC time (/TM) pilot observed the weather phenomena.
Flight level
The aircraft's altitude (/FL) in hundreds of feet is like METAR cloud heights. Decode
/FLUNKN as flight level unknown.
Type of aircraft
Read the aircraft type (/TP) without any modification.
Sky cover
The sky cover (/SC) at the pilot's flight level. Each layer has an amount as well as its base and
top in hundreds of feet, if known. Overcast layers may have an unknown base or top.
Weather
The weather (/WX) group contains the flight visibility (FV) indicator, visibility in two digits, its
units of measure, and any weather phenomenon. Decode visibility in whole miles with two
exceptions, unrestricted visibility (99) and visibility less than 1/2 mile (00).
The units of measure may be statute miles or kilometers. If statute miles, SM will follow the
visibility values. There is no indicator for kilometers which are normally only at non-United
States locations overseas.
Temperature
Decode temperature (/TA) in whole degrees Celsius. If the letter M precedes the temperature,
decode it as a negative number.
Winds
The wind (/WV) group has six digits. The first three are direction and the last three are the
speed.

Turbulence
The turbulence (/TB) group has four parts: duration, intensity, type, and altitude. The three
durations, four intensities, and two types combine to describe a multitude of turbulence
scenarios. For example, INTMT LGT CHOP is intermittent light chop turbulence and MOD
OCNL SEV CAT indicates moderate occasional severe clear air turbulence.
Duration:
- OCNL - Occasional
- INTMT - Intermittent
- CONS - Continuous
Intensity:
- LGT - Light
- MOD - Moderate
- SEV - Severe
- EXTRM - Extreme
Type:
- CAT - Clear Air Turbulence
- CHOP - Chop
Altitude has three formats, and you decode each one differently. First, when the PIREP has
turbulence without an altitude, the turbulence is at the aircraft's flight level. Second, it may have
the base and top of turbulence depicted in hundreds of feet using three digits. Third, the

- /TB INTMT MOD = Intermittent moderate turbulence at flight level.

- /TB LGT-MOD BLO 080 = Light to moderate turbulence below 8,000 feet.
The last turbulence entry is NEG, meaning the aircraft is in a forecast area of turbulence, but it
did not experience any turbulence.

The icing (/IC) group has four intensities and three types. Decode icing the same as the

Intensity:
- TRACE - Trace (amount too minimal to be properly identified)
- LGT - Light
- MOD - Moderate
- SEV - Severe
Type:
- RIME - Rime
- CLR - Clear
- MXD - Mixed

Isobars
Isobars are lines of equal pressure. They are horizontal brown lines drawn at 10 millibar (mb)
intervals and labeled every 50 millibars. Note the spacing between isobars is close at the bottom
and widens aloft. This is because the spacing reflects the expansion of our atmosphere as height
increases.

Isotherms
Isotherms are lines of equal temperature. They are diagonal brown lines extending from the
lower left to the upper right (Figure 1-2, right side). Their interval is one degree Celsius with
labels every five degrees. Also, there is a Fahrenheit scale along the bottom of the chart. You
can use it to convert temperatures to Fahrenheit when required.
Dry Adiabats
Dry adiabats are lines depicting the pressure and temperature change rate for rising or sinking
dry air. They are slightly curved, brown lines from the lower right to the upper left.

Saturation (Moist) Adiabats
Saturation adiabats are lines depicting the pressure and temperature change rate for rising or
sinking moist air. They are slightly curved, solid green lines from the lower right to the upper
left. Both saturation and dry adiabats parallel each other when the temperature is -55 degrees
Celsius or colder.
Wind Scales
The vertical staffs on the right side of the diagram depict wind speed and direction using wind
barbs. Each wind barb may have a number plotted next to it. The number represents the tens
unit for the wind direction.

Altitude Scale
On each side of the diagram, there will be an altitude scale measured in mb. Use the table data to
determine the height in feet based on the mb height on the diagram.
Standard Atmosphere Heights

SKEW-T Analysis Techniques
Temperature & Dew Point Lines
There are two basic lines plotted on a Skew-T from which we can derive much information.
These represent the dew point, which is calculated from the relative humidity (left line), and air
temperature (right line).
Freezing Level
The freezing level is the height of the zero-degree isotherm. More specifically, it is the height
where the temperature curve crosses the diagram's zero-degree isotherm. This may occur once
or at multiple levels. Determine the freezing level by starting at the surface temperature. Follow
the temperature curve upward until it crosses the zero-degree isotherm, then use the altitude scale
to obtain the freezing level height to the nearest 100 feet.
Available Moisture
You can identify the amount of available moisture at a location by using the areas dew-point
depression (DPD). The distance between the temperature and dew-point curves outlines layers
where there is enough moisture to fuel developing storms.
- 5+ (Dry)

- 3 to 4 (Moist)
- 1 to 2 (Wet)
- 0 (Fully Saturated)

Inversions
In the troposphere, temperature will generally decrease (cool) with height. Inversions describe
the areas of the atmosphere where the temperature increases (warms) with height. There are
certain features that cause this to happen. There will not always be an inversion, but when an
inversion is present, it is categorized as one of the following types.
Frontal: A frontal inversion is associated with surface fronts. The temperature and dew-point
curves warm (increase) with height.

Subsidence: A subsidence inversion is associated with downward vertical motion. Sinking air
adiabatically warms the troposphere creating the inversion. Dew-point depressions increase
causing the temperature and dew-point curves move away from each other.
Radiation: Radiation inversions form due to nighttime cooling. The main difference between it and the others is
radiation inversions are surface based meaning the base of the inversion is always at the Earth's surface. The
orientation of the dew-point curve does not matter. It may increase in height looking like a frontal inversion, or it
may decrease like a subsidence inversion.
- Near sunset the surface temperature begins to cool.
- Near midnight, the surface continues to cool as the earth eradiates the heat absorbed
during the day to create warmer temperatures above the surface and and a radiation
inversion develops.
- Before sunrise the surface temperature is at its coldest and the radiation inversion to reach
its maximum intensity.
- After sunrise the surface begins to absorb heat. This causes the surface temperature to
eventually become warmer than the atmosphere aloft. When the atmosphere is no longer
warming with height the inversion has dissipated.

Skew-T Indices
Convective Condensation level (CCL)
The CCL identifies the base of cumuliform clouds formed by free convection
Lifted Condensation Level (LCL)
The LCL identifies the base of stratiform clouds formed by forced convection.
Level of Free Convection (LFC)
The LFC is the height where air parcels are warmer than their environment. They can rise without
any atmospheric assistance enhancing upward vertical motion.
Equilibrium Level (EL)
The EL is the top of the LFC. Air parcels cool to match their surrounding atmosphere and no
longer rise on their own. It represents the top of a thunderstorm's updraft and often can be higher
than the tropopause.

Convective Available Potential Energy (CAPE)
CAPE is the measurement of how unstable the atmosphere is. If you remember in the last unit,
one of the ingredients needed for thunderstorm development was instability. The CAPE values
on a SKEW-T can give you a tangible number to correlate how unstable you're the atmosphere is
at a location. This is the area on a sounding between the temperature curve and the line
connecting the LFC to the EL along the moist adiabat curve. The largest CAPE will occur in the
warm sector of a mid-latitude cyclones, in the updraft region of a thunderstorm, an increase in
low level warm air advection, daytime heating, or upper-level cold air advection. The things that
cause atmospheric instability you learned previously will result in a higher CAPE value.
- 1 to 1,500 (Positive CAPE)
- 1,500 to 2,500 (Large CAPE)
- 2,500+ (Extreme CAPE)

Convective Inhibition (CINH)
CINH is the opposite of CAPE. CINH is a numerical value representing atmospheric stability, or
how much resistance to upward vertical motion there is. A dry boundary layer will lead to high
values of CINH and stability.
- Less than 50 (Low)
- 50-200 (Moderate)
- Above 200 (High)
Maximum Temperature
To determine the maximum temperature, you must use the cold Skew-T sounding. In the United
States that is at 12Z. You also need to know the forecast sky cover during the day. When the
forecast has broken to overcast skies, draw a line from the 850 mb temperature parallel to the

moist adiabats down to the surface. The temperature at that point is the maximum temperature in
Celsius. If the forecast is predominately clear to scattered skies, draw a line from the 850 mb
temperature parallel to the dry adiabats down to the surface. The temperature at that point is the
maximum temperature in Celsius.
Convective Temperature
Convective temperature is the temperature required for cumuliform clouds to form. If your
maximum temperature for the day reaches or exceeds the convective temperature, then daytime
heating and free convection will happen resulting in potential thunderstorm development. The
farther away your maximum temperature is from your convective temperature, the less likely
free convection is to occur. In this case, only forced convection would produce enough lift for
thunderstorm development.
Showalter Stability Inex (SSI)
This is the standard stability index for determining the potential for thunderstorm development.
- > +3 (Thunderstorms not likely)
- +3 to +1 (Thunderstorms Possible)
- 0 to -3 (Thunderstorms likely)
- -4 to -6 (Severe thunderstorms likely)

- < -6 (Tornadoes likely)
Total Totals (TT)
This index determines potential thunderstorm intensity.
- <44 (convection not likely)
- 44 to 50 (convection likely)
- 51 - 52 (isolated severe storms)
- 53 - 56 (widely scattered severe storms)
- >56 (scattered severe storms)
K-Index (KI)
The KI is primarily applicable in the prediction of air mass thunderstorms. Low values of the K
Index indicate low potential for air mass convection, while higher values indicate high potential.
- <15 (Convection not likely)
- 15 to 25 (Small potential for convection)
- 26 to 39 (Moderate potential for convection)

- 40+ (High potential for convection)
Lifted Index (LI)
The LI is the temperature difference between the environmental temperature at 500mb and the
air parcel temperature at 500mb. This calculation is another determination for the level of
instability in the atmosphere.
- 0 or greater (stable)
- -1 to -4 (marginal instability)
- -5 to -7 (large instability)
- -8 to -10 (extreme instability)
- -11 or less (ridiculous instability)
Hail & Wet-Bulb Zero (WBZ)
When predicting hail, the main factors to consider are the CAPE and the Wet-Bulb Zero height.
The CAPE determines the potential size of hailstones before they fall. Higher CAPEs lead to
hailstones being thrown higher vertical distances into the updraft.

The wet-bulb zero height is a function of how much dry air there is in the mid-levels of the
atmosphere. On a Skew-T, the larger the dewpoint depression, the higher your wet-bulb zero
height will be. Heights between 7,000 and 10,500 feet correlate well with large hail.
WBZ heights less than 10,500 feet will typically result in the Max Hail Index being the same or
close in value to the Expected Hail value. If WBZ heights are above 10,500 feet, there will be a
larger difference in those values due to the hail having more time to melt before reaching the
surface.
Precipitable Water (PW)
Value of precipitable water in inches. This is the total amount of water vapor in a vertical column
of the atmosphere.
- Greater than 1.75" represents a wet sounding.
- .75" to 1.75" represent a moist sounding.
- Less than 0.75" represents a dry sounding.

Thunderstorm Gust Speed
This shows gust speeds based on temperature differences and mean wind speeds. They can help
forecasters assess the severity of potential wind gusts associated with thunderstorms. There are
different methods designed to depict convective wind speeds and downbursts based on the type
of thunderstorm present or expected. All methods are depicted in the skew-t indices in knots of
potential gust speeds.
- T1 Gust - used for scattered or vicinity thunderstorms
- T2 Gust - used for intense squall lines and widespread thunderstorms
- Snyder Index - used for air-mass thunderstorms

Fetch
A Fetch is the uninterrupted horizontal distance over which wind blows across a body of water in
a constant direction, generating waves. The longer the fetch, the more energy the wind transfers
to the water, resulting in larger, more powerful, and more organized waves with greater height
and longer periods. Factors influencing fetch length include the size of the water body,
surrounding landmasses, and the wind's consistent direction and consistent speed.
Wind Waves
Wind waves are ripples on the ocean's surface created by localized winds and are referred to as
such while they remain within the region they were initially generated. The wave movement is
the same as the wind direction and wind speed determines the wave's height. Generically

speaking, light winds produce small waves, while stronger winds produce higher waves. The
time between consecutive waves is referred to as the "wave period" and is measured in seconds.
Swell Waves
Swell waves are long, low, and organized waves that form from energy transferred to the water
by wind waves, often from storms far out at sea. Their size depends on the residual;
uninterrupted energy created from the speed of winds associated with the storm.
Tsunami Waves
A tsunami is a series of extremely long waves caused by a large, sudden displacement of the
ocean such as an undersea earthquake or volcanic eruption.

Tides

Tides are cyclic rises and falls of seawater, influenced heavily by the moon and the sun.
Tide Types
The interaction between the Sun, Moon, and Earth as well as the Earth's land to water
distribution creates different variations in water levels along coastal areas. These water levels are
identified as a cycle of four transition periods:
High Tide: The period when water levels are at their highest and greatest expanse inland.
Ebb Tide: The period when high tide transitions to low tide as water begins to flow out to sea
Low Tide: The period when water levels are at their lowest and retreat the farthest offshore.
Flood tide: The period of when low tide transitions to high tide as water begins to flow towards
land.
Moon's Influence
The moon determines the tide day (a.k.a. tide period) and tide height. The tide day is 24 hours
and 50 minutes, which is equal to the time it takes the Moon to rotate around the Earth. During
this time, oceans experience up to two high tides and two low tides. Tide height changes based
on the Moon's position to the Earth and surface of the ocean.
High Tide: High tides occur when the Earth and Moon axis is parallel to the ocean. Oceans
"facing" the moon have a strong gravitational attraction and higher high tides.
Low Tide: Oceans facing away from the moon have a weak gravitational attraction and a lower
high tide.
Sun's influence
The Sun causes tide heights to increase and decrease. While its influence is minimal most of the
time, there are two instances the Sun causes greater changes in the tide height. These are during
spring and neap tides.
Spring Tide: Spring tides occur when the Sun and the Moon are parallel to Earth. This only
occurs every 14 to 15 days during a new or full Moon. During spring tides, the range between
tide heights increase creating high tides higher than average and low tides lower than average.
Neap Tide: Neap tides occur when the Sun and the Moon are perpendicular to the Earth. This
also only occurs every 14 to 15 days during the first and last quarter Moon. During neap tides,
the range between tide heights decrease creating high tides lower than average and low tides
higher than average.

Currents
Ocean currents are horizontal movements of seawater. Specifically, they are large features
extending across the oceans hundreds of miles wide and thousands of miles long.
Current Intensity

A current's intensity has two components. The first component is its speed or drift, which is
measured in knots. Conceptually, a fast drift is a strong current while a slow drift is a weak
current. The second component is the current's direction of movement or set using compass
points. For example, a current with a north set is moving towards 360 degrees while a current
with a west set is moving towards 270 degrees.
Temperature
Cold currents occur where colder water moves into warmer water. This occurs off the west
coasts of continents in the middle latitudes and off the east coasts of continents in the high
latitudes. The Canary, Labrador, and East Greenland currents are examples cold currents in the
Atlantic Ocean. Warm currents are off the east coast of continents in the middle latitudes and off
the west coast of continents in the high latitudes. In the Atlantic Ocean, the Gulf Stream and
Norwegian currents are warm currents.
Subtropical Gyres
Subtropical gyres are large oval or circular ocean currents near 30 degrees latitude. They are
permanent features that rotate anti-cyclonic in the Northern hemisphere and cyclonic in the
Southern hemisphere. There are five subtropical gyres. The North Pacific and North Atlantic
gyres in the northern hemisphere and the South Pacific, South Atlantic, and Indian Ocean gyres
in the southern hemisphere.
Coastal upwelling and downwelling
Coastal Upwelling: Coastal upwelling is the forced upward movement of cold, sub-surface water
to the ocean's surface. In the Northern Hemisphere, it occurs where northerly wind parallels the
coastline. The water along the coast turns to the right due to Earth's rotation which moves the
warm surface water away from the coast allowing colder subsurface water to rise and replace it.
Coastal downwelling: Coastal downwelling occurs where warm, surface water is forced to sink.
In the Northern Hemisphere, it occurs where southerly wind parallels the coastline. The water
along the coast turns to the right due to Earth's rotation which moves the surface water toward
the coast. This causes an accumulation of mass, and the weight of the water forces it to sink.

Littoral Zones
The littoral zone describes the sea state extending from the shoreline out to the point where the
ocean floor no longer has any impact to surface waters. No matter the size, the littoral zone is
separated into four distinct sub-zones: backshore, foreshore, nearshore, and offshore.

Backshore
The backshore is considered dry, unless influenced by extreme weather features such as hurricanes
or tsunamis. Located between the high tide mark and coastal vegetation that act as a protective
barrier for oceanographic events that may affect the coastline. It is characterized by dry sand and
pebbles.
Foreshore
The foreshore is the area of the beach ranging from low tide and high tide marks. This zone is
constantly shifting as waves change the shape of the beach and is characterized by wet sand or mud.
Nearshore
The nearshore is the area from the low tide mark to where waves begin to break (tops of waves
begin to white caps). The small area where waves begin to break is referred to as the surf zone.
Within the surface zone lies the longshore current, a current that runs parallel to the coast that can
force landing craft to broach, an involuntary change in a vessel's course. In the nearshore, there will
be sandbars and breaker bars formed by the interaction of waves.
Offshore
The offshore describes the area of deeper water beyond the point where waves start breaking and
have less interaction with the ocean floor, allowing for waves to maintain their regular shape and
size.
Breakers
As the swell waves move towards the nearshore environment, the interaction of the ocean floor and
the energy generated by the waves cause it to become unstable and break. Breaker height and
breaker type are the most important surf factors for determining the feasibility of an amphibious
beach operation. The interaction of the swell waves, slope of the ocean floor, wave period, and
surface winds will cause one of three types of breaker waves: Spilling, Plunging, and Surging. The
three types of breakers are described in the following.
Spilling: Spilling breakers normally occur on nearly horizontal beaches. The crest slides down the
face of the wave forming foam and gradually releasing energy over a wide area.

Plunging: The slope of the ocean floor is moderately steep, forcing the wave height to increase.
This instability causes the top of the wave to crash directly to the bottom. The crest plunges over
into the trough with a sudden release of energy over a narrow area.
Surging: Surging breakers are the least common type of breaker. The wave builds like a plunging
breaker, but the very steep-beach prevents breaking offshore. The steepness of the beach causes the
building breaker to surge onto shore with significantly more energy than a spilling breaker.
Longshore current: The longshore current is a current that runs parallel to the coast that can force
landing craft to broach, an involuntary change in a vessel's course. Imagine an East-West
running beach and Waves coming from the Southeast moving Northwest. The Longshore current
would be moving West parallelling the beach as the waves energy is forced to continue in that
direction.

Rip currents: Rip currents flow out from shore in narrow "rips" and are found on almost every open
coast. They are caused by the longshore currents converging near the shoreline and creating a new
current that is flowing away from shore toward open water. Once rip currents have formed, they
remain constant in position until the wave conditions change. Rip currents can be seen on imagery
as a calm area between breakers.

General Characteristics
Meteorologically, the tropics are between the northern and southern hemisphere's subtropical
highs. The tropics have a hot, humid climate with two seasons based on precipitation. The dry

season has a minimal amount of precipitation whereas the wet season has a maximum amount
of precipitation. In the North Hemisphere, the dry season is our winter and spring months while
the wet season is during our summer and autumn months.
Tropical Cyclones
Tropical cyclones are non-frontal, low-pressure centers in the tropics. They are associated with
rotating, organized cloud systems composed mostly of thunderstorms. They develop mostly
during the wet season. In the Atlantic, Hurricane season runs from June 1 - November 30. The
eastern Pacific season begins slightly earlier on May 15. The most active period within these
months is typically late summer and early fall, peaking around September.
Cyclogenesis: Tropical cyclogenesis requires a large oceanic area with all the following present:
- Weak vertical wind shear less than 20 knots
- Sea surface temperature of 26 degrees C or greater
- Warm water with a depth of 200 feet or more
- A pre-existing disturbance. In other words, any form of low-level convergence
- Must exist together poleward of 5 degreesN or 5 degreesS so Coriolis force can create a rotating
circulation.

Cyclolysis: The requirements for a tropical cyclone at any stage to undergo cyclolysis is the
opposite of its cyclogenesis. The main difference is you need all the ingredients for
cyclogenesis but only need one for cyclolysis.

development. They are tropical disturbance, tropical depression, tropical storm, and hurricane.
Tropical Disturbance. A tropical disturbance does not necessarily have a cyclonic center. It is an
organized area of thunderstorms generally 100 to 300 miles in diameter. It moves slowly in the
tropics from east to west and it can maintain its identity for 24 hours or longer.

Introduction to Space Weather
Earths Atmospheric Layers

Troposphere
The first and closest layer to the Earth's surface is the troposphere. The troposphere contains all
weather-producing meteorological features such as clouds and precipitation. Temperatures
within the troposphere cool with height meaning it is colder at the top of the layer than near the
Earth's surface. The top of the troposphere is the tropopause.
Stratosphere
The second layer of our atmosphere is the stratosphere, which contains the ozone layer. It
absorbs incoming ultraviolet radiation from the sun and heats the stratosphere meaning
temperatures warm with height. The stratopause is the top of the stratosphere.

Mesosphere
The mesosphere is the third layer of the atmosphere. It contains the Aurora, a natural light
display caused by charged solar particles entering our atmosphere near the North and South pole.
The light display in the northern hemisphere is the Aurora Borealis (northern lights) and in the
Southern hemisphere, it is the Aurora Australis (southern lights). Temperatures in the
mesosphere cool with height, and the top of the layer is the mesopause.
Thermosphere
The fourth layer is the thermosphere, and it contains the ionosphere, which has the greatest
number of electrically charged particles in the atmosphere. The ionosphere is used to reflect
radio waves back to earth. Temperature warms with height due to ultraviolet radiation, and the
top of the layer is the exobase.

Exosphere
The final layer is the exosphere. It gradually fades into outer space so there is no clear upper
boundary for the layer. It is more important to know the temperature cools with height, and it
allows atoms and molecules in our atmosphere to escape into outer space.
Space Weather Features
Insolation can significantly impact operations. The sun continuously emits electromagnetic (EM)
energy outward until it interacts with the Earth's atmosphere. These EM particles between the
Sun and Earth are known as the solar wind.
Solar Flare
A solar flare is a nuclear explosion on the sun's surface that usually occurs near a sunspot. The
flare releases high-energy particles into the solar wind.

Geomagnetic and Ionospheric Storm
Geomagnetic storms are rapid vibrations and variations in the Earth's magnetic field are caused
by the high-energy particles travelling along the solar wind. A geomagnetic storm may occur 24
to 72 hours after the solar flare and can last up to 3 days.

Space Weather Operational Impacts
High-frequency (HF) Communications
HF uses short-wave band radio waves generated by a transmitter. During transmission, the
ionosphere refracts HF radio waves to a receiving ground-based antenna. The ionosphere absorbs
frequencies, so they also do not refract to ground antennas resulting in a loss of communication.
The amount of HF impact varies based on seasonal changes, time of day, and geographical
location of the transmitting and receiving ground antennas. These change the amount of
incoming solar particles. Changes also occur with increased solar activity through solar flares,
increased geomagnetic activity, and variations in the solar environment.
Satellite Operations
Electrically charged particles emitted from the Sun may collide with satellites. The particles can
enter, reflect, or pass through the satellite. Internal satellite systems can short circuit resulting in
malfunctioning subsystems or false sensor readings. Worst case scenario is a possible loss of
altitude control causing the satellite to move out of orbit or completely shut down.
Global Positioning System (GPS)
Signals from GPS satellites travel through the ionosphere from the satellite to the receiver. The
ionosphere absorbs a part of the energy which causes distortion in the signal resulting in bad or
total signal loss. Potential failures include inaccurate positioning, incorrect navigation of aircraft
and ships, false orientation of ground-based units, and targeting errors for GPS guided munitions.

Prognosis Rules & Forecast Techniques
Prognosis rules & forecast techniques refer to the ability to use your knowledge of physics and
dynamics to predict the formation, dissipation, intensity changes, and movement of weather the
weather features you learned how to analyze previously. These rules and techniques can be
useful if you are working with limited data but still need to inform decision makers on potential
hazards. By applying some physics and dynamics, as well as previously observed patterns, you
can still have an informed forecast that can impact your mission.
A well-known forecast technique is, "The time in seconds between seeing lightning strikes and
hearing thunder will tell you the distance of the storm from your location in miles". This is a
rough estimate due to the differences in the speed of light vs speed of sound, but if that is the
only data you have, it is better than just guessing. Another useful forecast technique is, "one
contour/isobar per 100 miles produces wind speeds near 10 knots". So, if there were three
contours in the 100-mile area, the increasing gradient results in wind speeds near 30 knots. These
forecast techniques should be used as a general starting point to have an idea of what can be
expected. These should not be used on their own unless no other tools are available to you.

Anticyclogenesis
Anticyclogenesis describes the formation and building of anticyclonically oriented features.
Anticyclogenesis can be forecasted by analyzing:
Damper Effect: Convergence aloft, or divergence on the surface would result in downward
vertical motion. This would cause an increase in surface pressure resulting in anticyclogenesis.
Warm / Cold Air Advection: Increasing warm air advection upstream and/or cold air advection
downstream of an anticyclonically oriented feature would result in building of the anticyclonic
rotation resulting in anticyclogenesis.
Diabatic Effects: Surface based cold core features such as a high-pressure center moving over a
relatively colder surface would cause the cold air within the system to increase resulting in
anticyclogenesis.
Diurnal Effects: Surface based cold core features during night-time cooling would build in
intensity as the atmosphere affecting the feature would become relatively colder than during the
hottest part of the day resulting in a more stable atmosphere and anticyclogenesis.
Anticyclolysis
Anticyclolysis describes the dissipation and weakening of anticyclonically oriented features.
Anticyclolysis can be forecasted by analyzing:
Chimney Effect: Divergence aloft, or convergence at the surface would result in upward vertical
motion. This would cause surface pressure to decrease resulting in anticyclolysis.
Warm / Cold Air Advection: Increasing cold air advection upstream and/or warm air advection
downstream of an anticyclonically oriented feature would result in weakening the anticyclonic
rotation resulting in anticyclolysis.
Diabatic Effects: Surface based cold core features such as a high-pressure center moving over a
relatively warmer surface would cause the cold air within the system to begin absorbing the heat
and decreasing surface pressure resulting in anticyclolysis.
Diurnal Effects: Surface based cold core features during daytime heating would decrease in
intensity as the atmosphere affecting the feature would become relatively warmer than during
nigh-time cooling resulting in a decrease in pressure and anticyclolysis.
Cyclogenesis
Cyclogenesis describes the formation and deepening of cyclonically oriented features.
Cyclogenesis can be forecasted by analyzing:
Chimney Effect: Divergence aloft, or convergence at the surface would result in upward vertical
motion. This would cause surface pressure to decrease, resulting in cyclogenesis.

Warm / Cold Air Advection: Increasing cold air advection upstream and/or warm air advection
downstream of a cyclonically oriented feature would result in deepening the cyclonic flow
resulting in cyclogenesis.
Diabatic Effects: Surface based warm core features such as a low-pressure center moving over a
relatively warmer surface would cause the colder air within the system to begin absorbing the
heat and decreasing surface pressure resulting in cyclogenesis.
Diurnal Effects: Surface based warm core features during daytime heating would increase in
intensity and deepen as the atmosphere affecting the feature would become relatively warmer
than during nigh-time cooling resulting in a decrease in pressure and cyclogenesis.
Cyclolysis
Cyclolysis describes the dissipation and filling of cyclonically oriented features.
Cyclolysis can be forecasted by analyzing:
Damper Effect: Convergence aloft, or divergence on the surface would result in downward
vertical motion. This would cause an increase in surface pressure resulting in cyclolysis.
Warm / Cold Air Advection: Increasing warm air advection upstream and/or cold air advection

cyclolysis.
Diabatic Effects: Surface based warm core features such as a low-pressure center moving over a
relatively colder surface would cause the pressure center to release heat and increasing pressure
resulting in cyclolysis.
Diurnal Effects: Surface based warm core features during night-time cooling would decrease in
intensity as the atmosphere affecting the feature would become relatively colder than during the
hottest part of the day resulting in cyclolysis.

Cloud Forecast Techniques
Clouds form when water vapor changes to either liquid droplets or ice crystals. This
requires an atmospheric cooling process. Adiabatic cooling from free or forced
convection is the most effective cooling process.
Convection and stability determine the type of cloud and precipitation. An unstable
atmosphere (cold over warm temperatures) will develop cumuliform clouds, while a
stable atmosphere (warm over cold temperatures) will develop stratiform clouds.
available moisture is more important than stability for cloud development.

Lifting Mechanism
There are four main types of lifting mechanisms: orographic, frontal, low-level
convergent flow, and surface heating.
Orographic: Orographic lift is the most effective and intense cooling process. The
overall effect is dependent on the change from horizontal surface flow to vertical motion
proportional to the terrain's slope. The strongest or greatest amount of lift occurs with a
steep slope, while a gradual or relatively flat slope has the weakest amount of orographic
lift.
Frontal: Frontal lift occurs with all fronts. Steep slopes (cold fronts) produce convective
cloud types, while a shallow slope (warm and stationary fronts) produce widespread
stable clouds.
Low-Level Confluent Flow: Low-level confluent flow is common in the warm sector of a
baroclinic low ahead of an approaching cold front. Enhanced cumuliform clouds
develop when low-level confluent flow combines with diffluent upper-level flow.

Surface Heating: Surface heating produces convective currents which may produce
cumuliform clouds. Cloud formation may be random and isolated at inland locations, or
it may organize into a linear form along a sea or land breeze.
Stratiform
Stratiform clouds form within stable atmospheric layers.
Stratus (ST). There must be cooling in the surface moist layer for stratus clouds to form.
They are typical with warm air masses and are normally 1,000 to 4,000 feet thick with
basses less than 2,000 feet above ground level (AGL). Conditions favorable for
development include:
- Warm frontal zones where warm, moist air are advected over a cooler surface.
Clouds form near the front due to slight upward vertical motion.
- Along gradual upslope conditions due to orographic lift.
- Areas of weak surface confluence with high moisture content.
Stratus clouds dissipate by removing moisture from the atmosphere. Thin layers typically
do not take long to dissipate, but thicker layers take longer since they inhibit insolation

(surface heating). Warmer surface temperatures can absorb the moisture thereby
dissipating the stratus.
Altostratus (AS). Altostratus clouds are associated with wave cyclones. They are
poleward of the warm front with unstable waves and poleward of the warm front as well
as along the western edge of a comma cloud.
Cirrostratus (CS). Cirrostratus forms with well-developed wave cyclones normally 700
to 1,000 miles ahead of a warm front. The clouds may move with the gradient flow days
after the frontal system dissipates.
Cirrus (CI). Cirrus is often associated with the remnants of a cumulonimbus cloud and
can remain in the atmosphere after the storm collapses.
Cumuliform Clouds
Cumuliform clouds form in unstable environments. The cloud types include cumulus,
towering cumulus, and cumulonimbus. Their bases vary based on the lifting mechanism.
When moist air rises mechanically by frontal or orographic lift, cloud bases form near
the lifted condensation level (LCL). When the air rises thermally from surface heating,
cloud bases are closer to the convective condensation level (CCL).

Cumuliform tops are dependent on the height of the instability layer. Their vertical
development ends at the equilibrium layer (EL). This is where air parcels' temperature
and pressure equal the environment therefore causing vertical motion to stop. The
equilibrium layer may be a stable isothermal layer, an inversion, or the tropopause.
Frontal Clouds
Warm Fronts: Warm frontal clouds are generally stratiform unless the atmosphere is
unstable. Embedded cumulus may occur in those instances. Another characteristic of
warm frontal clouds is based on the 700-mb flow over the frontal boundary. When the
flow is cyclonic or straight-line, embedded cumulus may form and when it is
anticyclonic, there is less potential for clouds and precipitation poleward of the frontal
boundary.
Inactive Cold Fronts: Most clouds form on the leading edge (downstream side) of
inactive cold fronts perpendicular to the 700 mb flow. The front has the steepest slope

and moves the fastest at an average speed of 25 knots causing rapid clearing of all clouds
after frontal passage.
Active Cold Fronts: Most clouds form at and behind (upstream of) active cold fronts.
The cloud shield is more extensive since the frontal slope is lower and its speed (average
15 knots) is slower than an inactive cold front. The overall result is clouds clear slowly
after frontal passage.
Cloud Bases and Precipitation
Precipitation lowers cloud bases. Continuous precipitation of sufficient duration can also
lower cloud bases below 2,000 feet often by 800 feet. However, the actual height of the
cloud base can vary depending on the location, terrain, and latitudinal differences.
Showery or heavy precipitation can also rapidly drop cloud bases below 2,000 feet.
Clouds Dissipation
Clouds dissipate by removing moisture through dry air advection, adiabatic warming
(downward vertical motion), or removing the lifting mechanism.
Cold Frontal Clouds: Cloud layers will dissipate or move with the frontal system in the
following order of events:

- Low etage clouds begin to move and dissipate with passage of the 850-mb upper
front and they completely move away with passage of the 700-mb frontal trough.
- Middle etage clouds begin to move and dissipate with passage of the 700-mb
frontal trough and they completely move away with passage of the 500-mb
frontal trough.
- High etage clouds begin to move and dissipate with passage of the 500-mb
frontal trough and they completely move away with passage of the 500-mb
thermal trough.
Icing Forecast Techniques
Effects of Aircraft Icing
Icing increases drag and weight of an aircraft, while decreasing its lift capabilities. Other
structural effects include loss of aircrew's vision, instrument failure, and loss of radio
communication. Engine-system icing reduces the effective power of aircraft engines, potentially
causing them to stall and stop working.

Formation Requirements
Icing formation requires suspended super-cooled water droplets with temperatures below
freezing and moisture available. Regarding temperature, most icing forms between 0 degrees C and -
22 degrees C. Supercooled water droplets can form between -23 degrees C and -40 degrees C, however, droplets with
temperatures less than -40 degrees C are completely frozen. So, icing does not exist with temperatures
below -40 degrees C.
Severity
There are definite criteria defining a pilot's reactions for icing during flight operations. Be aware
of the icing effects differ between aircraft type. The differences include fixed versus rotary wing
aircraft, their operational flight altitudes, and available de-icing equipment. For example, de-
icing equipment is not available on all military aircraft. To differentiate between the two,
deicing equipment melts ice on the aircraft during flight and anti-icing procedures prevents ice
accumulation which is conducted on the runway prior to takeoff.

Trace
The first level occurs when ice becomes perceptible meaning the pilot recognizes it is on the
aircraft. De-icing equipment or anti-icing procedures are not necessary. Trace icing is not
hazardous unless the aircraft encounters the icing for more than one hour.
Light
Light icing occurs when ice accumulates on the aircraft. The rate of accumulation is slow and not
a hazard unless the aircraft is in the condition for more than one hour. Occasional use of deicing
equipment or anti-icing procedures are required.

Moderate
The rate of ice accumulation with a moderate intensity is quick making short encounters
hazardous to aircraft operations. De-icing equipment or anti-icing procedures are necessary for
flight safety and diversion may be required if the de-icing equipment fails.
Severe
An environment with severe icing occurs when de-icing equipment does not reduce ice
accumulation. Immediate diversion is necessary to continue aircraft operations.
Types
Clear Icing
Clear icing forms when super-cooled water droplets gradually freeze when striking an aircraft.
This is usually associated with precipitation since it begins with large, super-cooled water
droplets. The unfrozen water droplet flows or smears over the airframe and freezes. The ice
accumulates on the aircraft as a thin, smooth surface. Clear icing is hard and heavy making it
very difficult to remove.

Rime Icing
Rime icing forms by the instantaneous freezing of small, super- cooled water droplets which
traps large amounts of air within the ice. It is associated with super-cooled cold droplets within
warm clouds. Rime icing has a milky, opaque, and granular appearance. It is also lightweight
and brittle making it easy to remove.
Mixed Icing
Mixed icing is a combination of rime and clear icing. It is formed most often where cloud and
precipitation droplets intermix. These areas have water droplets varying in liquid water content,
droplet size, and temperature. It appears as layers of clear and opaque ice due to ice particles
embedding into the smoother clear ice as it freezes on the aircraft. Overall, it has a rough
appearance in thin layers with embedded air pockets.
Icing and Cloud Type

Stratiform Clouds
Low and middle etage stratiform clouds are associated with mixed and rime icing. Their
intensity ranges from light to moderate.
Cumuliform Clouds
Clear or mixed icing is always present in the updrafts of a cumulonimbus cloud. Light icing
occurs with small cumulus clouds and moderate icing is within towering cumulus clouds. Severe
icing is always associated with cumulonimbus clouds.
Cirriform Clouds
Icing rarely occurs with cirriform clouds. The exception is the dense cirrus and anvil tops of a
cumulonimbus cloud where there is a considerable amount of water in the updraft.
Frontal Systems
85% of reported icing conditions occur with frontal systems with the greatest amount associated
with warm and cold fronts.
Warm Fronts
Icing forms on the poleward side of a warm front in a widespread area. Light rime icing occurs
in altostratus clouds up to 300 miles ahead of the warm front surface. Light mixed icing occurs
along the frontal slope where clouds and precipitation mix. The icing layer may extend 100 to
200 miles downstream of the front. Lastly, clear icing occurs in precipitation and cold air below
the warm frontal slope. Intensities can vary based on the precipitation type.
Cold Fronts
Icing forms in narrow regions with cold fronts due to less extensive cloud coverage.
Stratocumulus clouds behind the cold front have light mixed icing when precipitation is present
or light rime when there is no precipitation. Icing also develops in cumulonimbus and towering
cumulus clouds up to 100 miles from the frontal boundary. The area may be upstream of an

active cold front or downstream of an inactive cold front. Regardless of location, there are three
potential icing combinations.
- Moderate clear icing with average temperatures between 0 and -08 degrees C
- Moderate mixed icing with average temperatures between -09 and -15 degrees C
- Moderate rime icing with average temperatures between -16 and -22 degrees C.

Wind Forecast Techniques
Wind flows from high to low pressure. High-pressure centers have anticyclonic flow and a weak
isobaric gradient resulting in slower surface wind speeds. Low-pressure centers have cyclonic
flow and a tighter isobaric gradient resulting in faster wind speeds.
Synoptic Winds
Synoptic winds stay consistent with the pressure system's forecast meaning they are directly
proportional to the approaching pressure center. High pressure centers cause wind direction to
veer anticyclonically, while low pressure centers cause wind direction to back cyclonically.
Speed is proportional to the pressure gradient. A loose or decreasing isobaric gradient results in
slower speeds, while a tight or increasing isobaric gradient results in faster speeds.
Diurnal Trends
Consider diurnal trends in every forecast. If the air mass does not change, then the winds should
be nearly the same as the previous day's wind direction and speed. They decrease at night due to
atmospheric cooling and formation of a radiation inversion. Surface winds have low speeds
below the radiation inversion and stronger winds above the radiation inversion. The lightest
surface winds occur near sunrise when the inversion is the strongest. Daytime heating initiates

free convection after sunrise and breaks the inversions by mid-morning resulting in higher wind
speeds.
Sea Breeze
Sea breezes occur during the day. They form when cooler air over water moves over warmer
land with a weak pressure gradient. They begin to develop 3 to 4 hours after sunrise and often
extends 10 miles inland but may extend as far as 30-40 miles inland. Speeds average between 10
and 20 knots.
Land Breeze
Land breezes occur during the night. They form when cooler air over land moves over warmer
water with a weak pressure gradient. They develop after midnight and reach their maximum
speed slightly before sunrise. Forecast wind direction offshore perpendicular to the coastline.
Speeds average 10 knots.

Upslope Winds
Upslope winds occur during the day or night. They form when the isobaric pattern is
perpendicular to a mountain or mountain range. Forecast wind speeds are based on the pressure
gradient. Tighter gradients produce strong wind speeds and possible upslope clouds. Weaker
gradients result in light wind speeds with upslope stratus. Upslope winds cease or never form
when the synoptic isobaric pattern is parallel to the mountain range.
Downslope Winds
Downslope winds form with a nearly perpendicular synoptic contour pattern along the mountain
range in the upper levels. Forecast wind direction based on the contour gradient crossing the
mountain tops. Strong wind speeds are near mountain passes and downstream parallel to the
surface isobaric gradient. Speeds can reach 100 knots when channeled (funneled).
Turbulence Forecast Techniques
Turbulence is any irregular motion of air such as random and continuously changing air direction
and speeds within the synoptic flow pattern. Turbulence with fixed wing aircraft is directly
proportional to aircraft speed and wing area but inversely proportionate to weight. Turbulence on
rotary wing aircraft are directly proportional to their speed and rotary blades but inversely
proportionate to weight and lift velocities.

Categories
There are six turbulence categories. They are:
- Light turbulence - slight erratic changes in altitude and/or attitude (stability at flight
level)
- Light chop - slight, rapid rhythmic bumpiness without any appreciable changes in altitude
and/or attitude.
- Moderate turbulence - small changes in altitude and/or attitude but the pilot always stays
in positive control of the aircraft. Usually indicated by variations in air speed.
- Moderate chop - rapid bumps or jolts without changes in altitude and/or attitude.
- Severe turbulence - large changes in altitude and/or attitude and aircraft is difficult to
control. Usually indicated by large variations in air speed,
- Extreme turbulence - large sudden changes in altitude and/or attitude. Aircraft is violently
tossed around and virtually impossible to control. Structural damage may occur.
Turbulence Types
Convective (Thermal). Convective turbulence occurs where isolated vertical air currents form
due to surface heating. We know warm air rises and cold air sinks. When these are next to each
other they produce bumpy conditions, pilots refer to as CHOP. Convective turbulence affects the
lower atmosphere typically from surface to 10,000 feet. It occurs most often in the late morning
through late afternoon. Favorable conditions for its formation include warm summer afternoons
with clear to partly cloudy skies, light surface winds, and uneven terrain with different types of
surfaces. Its intensity is normally light to moderate but can be moderate to severe when
associated with thunderstorms.
Convective

Mechanical Turbulence. Wind flowing over irregular terrain or through and around obstacles
creates mechanical turbulence. Its intensity depends on a variety of factors:
- Roughness of terrain
- Height, configuration, and specific density of obstructions
- Wind speeds
- Air mass stability.
The mechanical turbulence layer is usually 2,000 feet thick, 10 to 40 miles wide, and can extend
hundreds of miles long. Actual numbers will vary, but mechanical turbulence areas are always
longer than they are wide. Favorable conditions require a strong horizontal pressure gradient,
irregular and mountainous terrain, frontal zones, or radiation inversions.
- Strong pressure gradients create stronger wind speeds which increases turbulent mixing
near the surface creating mechanical turbulence.
Winds from Strong Pressure Gradient
- Irregular or mountainous terrain, especially with greater terrain irregularity or steeper
slopes produces higher turbulence intensities due to a grater vertical extent of the
turbulent mixing.
Irregular Surface
- Warm sector winds force air aloft along the frontal slopes of an upstream cold front and
downstream of warm fronts.

Frontal
- Larger currents occur with inactive cold fronts and smaller currents occur with warm
fronts.
- Radiation inversions have lighter surface winds trapped below the inversion, while
stronger wind speeds are aloft above the inversion. Mechanical turbulence forms along
the frontal zones or the radiation inversion's boundaries.
Radiation Inversion
Low-level Wind Shear (LLWS). LLWS is a form of low-level mechanical turbulence. It has a
specific definition which makes it a separate turbulence type. LLWS occurs with abrupt changes
in wind speed and/or direction within 2,000 feet of the surface associated with airspeed changes
greater than 15 knots and/or vertical speed changes greater than 500 feet per minute. It is
extremely dangerous for fixed and rotary wing aircraft during take-off and landing as well as
during flights below 2,000 feet AGL. These operations are too close to the surface so if the
aircraft experiences LLWS, the pilot may be unable to react appropriately and may result in
crashing into the aircraft. Locations of LLWS include:
- Near Thunderstorms: This exists horizontally along outflow boundaries and can extend
10 to 20 miles from the thunderstorm. It also exists vertically between updrafts and
downdrafts in thunderstorms.
- Frontal Boundaries: The strongest intensities will occur with an inactive cold front from
frontal passage and up to 2 hours behind the cold front. With warm fronts, is weaker than
cold fronts but the wind shear area extends up to 6 hours ahead of a warm frontal
passage.
- Low-Level Jet: Forms with a low-level jet above a radiation inversion. In this instance,
winds below the inversion are typically less than 10 knots and winds above the inversion

are 30 knots or more. Speed changes and vertical distance determines the intensity of
LLWS.
Low Level Wind Shear
Mountain Wave Turbulence. Mountain wave turbulence extends downstream of mountain
ranges. It is commonly associated with higher mountains but can exist in any mountain range. It
can extend as high as 70,000 feet and typically extends as far as 300 miles downstream from the
mountain range. The strongest turbulent areas occur with a strong perpendicular wind component
to the mountain range. Favorable conditions for formation include a stable layer at and above the
mountain tops, a subsidence inversion compressing the atmosphere vertically, and downward
vertical motion combining with the perpendicular wind component.
- If the perpendicular wind component within 2,000 feet of the mountain tops and the wind
speed is 25-49 knots, forecast severe turbulence up to 50 miles downstream and moderate
turbulence 50 to 150 miles downstream of the mountain range.
- When the perpendicular wind component is 50 knots or more, forecast extreme
turbulence up to 10 miles, severe turbulence between 10 and 150 miles, and moderate
turbulence from 150 to 300 miles downstream of the mountain range.
Mountain Wave Turbulence
Wind
Clear Air Turbulence (CAT). Clear air turbulence forms in clear air. It is typically an elongated
area of strong winds forming more frequently during the winter. Atmospheric conditions
favorable for CAT are:
- Anticyclonic oriented jet stream. Forecast moderate CAT symmetrical to the jet axis
when it flows around a medium to high-amplitude long-wave ridge axis. The turbulent
area extends 250 miles on both sides of the ridge axis.

- Areas of deep troughs or sharp ridges. Forecast moderate to severe CAT when winds
exceed 135 knots with large curvature or winds exceed 115 knots with sharp curvature.
- Developing cut-off lows. Forecast moderate CAT in the opposing winds and subsequent
wind shear zones associated with the confluent or diffluent contours poleward of a low-
height center or 500-mb cold pocket.
- Closed surface lows undergoing cyclogenesis. CAT forms near the jet core height north
to northeast of a developing surface low-pressure center. Turbulence intensity is based
on the rate of cyclogenesis.

Precipitation Forecast Techniques

Formation Requirements
Precipitation formation requirements include ample moisture, and instability. The
process begins by forming a cloud near or moving towards an ample moisture source.
Increased moist air advection feeds the cloud especially if the synoptic flow becomes
more perpendicular from the moisture source to the cloud's position. Increasing
instability is also a crucial factor whether it is from a lifting mechanism such as surface
convergence or divergence aloft.
Precipitation-cloud Relationships
Precipitation cloud relationships are complex and different types of clouds can produce
different types of precipitation. A quick synopsis of each includes:
- Cumulonimbus clouds are associated with showery precipitation that may include
rain, snow, or ice pellets, and hail.
- Cumulus clouds must reach a vertical development of 8,000 to 10,000 feet or
more with the cloud tops reaching heights of -12 to -20 degrees C or colder. Precipitation
types may be rain, snow, or ice pellets.
- Stratocumulus clouds typically produce light rain, snow, or snow pellets. If the
cloud base is low drizzle can occur.
- Stratus clouds may produce light to heavy drizzle, rain, snow, and snow grains.
- Altostratus clouds produce continuous rain, snow, or ice pellets with moderate
size drops. Precipitation seldom reaches the ground if the cloud base is above
10,000 feet.
- Altocumulus clouds are typically not associated with precipitation.
Precipitation Enhancers
Precipitation enhancers are substances or processes that increase the likelihood of
precipitation. The most common enhancers include:
- Moist air advection increases relative humidity.
- Cold air advection decreases the atmosphere's ability to hold moisture thereby
increasing relative humidity. This occurs the most behind cold fronts or during
nighttime cooling.
- Diffluent or divergent flow aloft increases upward vertical motion bringing
warmer, moist air to colder air aloft.

Visibility Forecast Techniques
Fog
Fog is a "cloud" forming below 50 feet and usually forms on the surface but can form aloft then build
down to the surface. It is the most common visibility restrictor which can reduce visibility down to
zero. Dissipation of fog can occur any one of three ways: remove or decrease moisture, heat the
atmosphere, or change the surface wind speed.
Formation
Fog needs three things for formation and all three must be present or fog never develops.
- Increased Moisture. You must have an abundant amount of moisture for fog to form.
- Atmospheric Cooling. The air needs to cool so relative humidity increases toward 100%
or higher, forcing condensation to occur.
- Turbulent Mixing. For fog formation turbulent mixing must be present between the
surface and boundary layer. Wind speeds must be high enough to increase moisture
transport and support cold air advection, and they must be low enough to aid in fog
formation. If the turbulent mixing is too much or too strong it will be difficult to achieve
saturation due to mixing drier ambient air. Conversely if it is too low or too weak
moisture remains on the surface forming dew or frost depending on surface temperature.
Radiation Fog
The first item needed is moist air trapped below a radiation inversion. Moisture may be from
surface water sources like a lake or river, residual water from precipitation, or higher levels of
relative humidity due to moist air advection. The second requirement is surface winds between
3-7KT. Clear skies overnight enhance radiation fog since it maximizes nighttime cooling.
Radiation Fog

Advection Fog
When warm, moist air moves over colder surfaces, the temperature drops due to diabatic cooling.
This occurs most near lake or large river shorelines or coastal waters. It can occur inland as well
where warm or moist air advection flows over continental or modified continental polar air. In
addition to direction, surface wind speeds are also a factor. When winds are less than 3 knots,
the advection fog tends to be shallow and if the speed is between 3 and 9 knots it is deep, dense
fog.
Frontal Fog
The three requirements for frontal fog formation are moist air forced aloft by a frontal boundary,
evaporative cooling, and light surface winds. This can occur in two locations producing two types of
frontal fog. Frontal fogs rarely dissipate. In most cases they simply move downstream with
the surface fronts.
Pre-frontal Fog. Pre-frontal or warm-frontal fog is the most common type. It is a widespread
area located in the cold air ahead of warm fronts often underneath a baroclinic leaf or comma
cloud on satellite imagery.
Post-frontal fog. Post-frontal or cold frontal fog occurs less often since it is behind surface cold
fronts. Since it requires evaporative cooling, it make sense that it occurs most often 150 to 250
miles behind active cold fronts. The exact location is between the surface front and 850-mb
upper cold front.
Upslope Fog
Upslope fog forms when surface winds force moist air aloft by orographic lift. The winds perpendicular
component to the higher terrain must have speeds less than 10KT. The closer the wind direction is to

perpendicular increases the potential of upslope fog formation. Initially the fog forms at higher elevations
then builds downward. If the condition extends over time, upslope fog may change to upslope stratus.
Blowing Dust or Sand
Blowing dust or sand is a function of wind speed and direction as well as unprotected, loose soil
or sand. Essentially blowing dust or sand can occur with any weather feature creating low-level
confluent or convergence upstream of an area with loose soil or sand.
Areas with wind speed greater than or equal to 15 knots are needed. Lower speeds rarely lift the
soil or sand above six feet AGL. Additionally, the surface dew-point depression may be greater
than or equal to 10 degrees C.
Blowing Snow
The requirements for blowing snow are essentially the same as those for blowing dust or sand.
The main difference is loose snow is needed, preferably freshly fallen snow. Winds must be
strong enough to lift the snow above six feet AGL. 10 to 15 knots will lift loose, dry snow off the
ground but to lift moderate, dry, fluffy snow wind speed must be 15 knots or higher.

- Minimum temperatures occur approximately one hour before or after sunrise.

Airmass Changes
Frontal Passage. After frontal passage temperatures change based on the frontal system that
passed through your forecast location. Temperatures increase immediately after the passage of

warm front. Cold fronts are different although temperatures cool gradually after their passage.
The coldest temperature after an inactive cold front occurs an average of 12 to 24 hours after

hours after passage.
Thermal Advection. Thermal advection will modify temperatures whether the air mass changes
or whether it stays the same. With surface warm air advection, the boundary layer destabilizes,
temperatures increase, and cumuliform clouds may form. Cold air advection is the opposite. The
boundary layer stabilizes, temperatures decrease, and stratiform clouds with possible and light
precipitation may develop.
Pressure Forecast Techniques
Surface Pressure
Surface pressure is the atmospheric pressure at a specific location. It is directly proportional to
the air mass above a location. You know the global standard for sea-level pressure is 1013 mb or
29.92 inches of mercury (in Hg).
Diurnal Pressure Cycle
If there is no change in airmass, pressure rises and falls diurnally. Lower pressure is directly
proportional to daytime heating, and higher pressure is directly proportional to nighttime cooling.
The pressure rarely exceeds 3 mb daily.

Migrating Weather Systems
To forecast pressure with migrating weather systems, you must first forecast the movement and
intensity change in the approaching pressure center and modify the isobaric pattern due to terrain
influences. When a lower pressure system approaches, you should forecast lower pressure and
consider increasing wind speeds with an increasing isobar gradient, increasing temperatures with
southerly winds, decreasing stability, and increasing cloud coverage with precipitation possible.
When a high-pressure system approaches, forecast increasing pressure and stability with
decreasing wind speeds associated with a loosening isobar gradient. Any considerations after that
depend on the air mass characteristics associated with the higher-pressure system.
Temperatures will decrease with a polar air mass after the cold front passes, or they will increase
as maritime tropical air replaces retreating cold air. With a polar air mass expect fog, stratus, or
stratocumulus to form after cold frontal passage then eventually decrease to clear skies.
A maritime tropical air mass brings an increased chance of cumuliform cloud development or an
increased subsidence inversion that may trap pollutants near surface bringing the visibility down
due to haze or smoke.

All pressure forecast techniques compute or determine pressure forecasts using sea-level
pressure in millibars.

Radar Introduction & Characteristics
Introduction
There are multiple radar networks throughout the world. Each one is different, but before you
get too far into that you need to understand it is the best tool we have for identifying the location
and intensity of convective and non-convective severe weather.
The goal of this objective will be to show you have the capability to interpret radar imagery to
identify and label the areas of severe weather. To successfully demonstrate competency on this
topic, you will be given information through lecture, a series of questions and tasks designed to
display your ability to correctly navigate a weather radar and identify significant radar returns
discussed throughout this objective. These foundational forecasting concepts will be measured
with a Progress Check (PC) at the end of the objective and must be passed to continue onto the
next objective. Follow your instructor's guidance as they provide information and instruction for
completing all requirements necessary to pass this objective.
Volume Coverage Pattern (VCP)
A Volume Coverage Pattern (VCP) is a systematic approach to scan the atmosphere using a
radar antenna horizontally and vertically. Horizontally the antenna scans in a 360 degrees circle and
vertically it scans different elevations from .5 degrees to 19.5 degrees. Volume coverage patterns contain data
gaps depicting atmospheric regions that are not scanned by the radar. They are narrow regions
between elevation scans with a larger region above 19.5 degrees known as the cone of silence.
Electromagnetic Energy (EM)
The radar uses electromagnetic (EM) energy to determine the ability to resolve targets. If you
recall, EM energy's structure is a sine wave consisting of a series of troughs and ridges. The
distance from one trough to another or one ridge to another is the radar's wavelength. Short
wavelengths detect small targets such as cloud formations or light precipitation while longer
wavelengths detect larger targets such as heavy precipitation or hail.

Single Polarization Radar
Single polarization radars are either or horizontally or vertically polarized. A single polarized
radar can detect the location, movement, and certain features within a scan but are limited in the
precise measurements that dual-polarized radars can offer.
Dual Polarization Radars
Dual polarization radars transmit and detect horizontally and vertically polarized waves
simultaneously. Dual polarization detection improves our capability to detect weather
phenomena by providing higher quality data.

Beam Characteristic
Beam Broadening EM energy leaves the antenna from a single point in a small narrow beam. As
the beam travels further away from the radar, it will increase its width and height with distance.
The further the beam travels the bigger it gets, giving it a conical appearance. 50 miles from the
radar, the beam is approximately one mile wide.
Beam Width
Beam width is the area within the cone shaped beam. The greatest amount of energy travels
along the beam axis like a flashlight. The greatest clarity is near the center of the circle or along
its beam axis.
Beam Refraction
Refraction is a deflection or bending of the radar beam caused by atmospheric density differences.
There are four types of beam refraction.
Standard Refraction: The beam's curvature is slightly less than the earth's curvature. It will bend
slightly skyward the farther from the radar you get.
Sub Refraction: A form of anomalous (non-standard) propagation where the beam bends less than
standard refraction causing the beam to climb excessively skyward.
Super Refraction: A form of anomalous propagation where the beam bends more than standard
refraction forcing the beam to bend excessively earthward.
Ducting: An extreme form of super refraction. A low-level inversion traps the radar signal
causing it to ricochet between the surface and the inversion. Ducting causes the beam to travel
greater distances than normal.

Beam Blockage
Beam blockage or ground clutter occurs when the beam strikes fixed objects such as buildings,
trees, or uneven terrain. The objects fill greater portions of the beam so when energy strikes
them, they return more energy to the antenna than the atmospheric features surrounding them.
Ground clutter generally occurs near the antenna but can occur at great distances with large
mountain ranges, super refraction, or ducting.

Velocity
Weather radars can detect wind direction and speed based on the amount of motion detected
along the beam axis, otherwise called radial velocity. This may sound easy but there are three
possible scenarios.
- Motion parallel to the beam axis detects the actual wind speed and direction.
- Motion perpendicular to the beam axis detects zero velocity and assumes the system is
stationary.
- Motion crossing the beam axis diagonally detects wind speed less than the actual wind
speed. The closer to perpendicular the system, the slower the wind speed will appear.
Atmospheric Interactions
The radar antenna continuously transmits EM energy. The energy travels through the
atmosphere interacting with ground clutter and a variety of other scatterers. A scatterer is any
airborne object that is large enough to affect EM energy's propagation. Reflection is necessary
for target detection reliable data occurs when a pulse strikes an atmospheric particle and energy
is deflecting back to the radar.
Attenuation
Attenuation is a loss or reduction in power resulting in unreliable radar data.
Absorption: Scatterers absorb EM energy resulting in a permanent loss of data. The radar cannot
detect any target beyond the scatterer.

Mie scattering: Scatterers radiate energy forward away from the antenna. This is also a
permanent loss of energy and undetected targets.
Rayleigh scattering: Scatterers deflect energy sideways. The lost energy may be permanent or
partial. Permanent loss occurs when energy deflects sideways and away from the antenna.
Partial loss occurs when the energy deflects sideways and towards the antenna resulting in target
detection.
Radar Products
Radar products are radar images depicting a variety of data in a 360 degrees circle for a single elevation.
During this course, we will look at four base products from a single polarized radar. The products
include Base Reflectivity (BR), Base Velocity (BV), Echo Tops (ET), and Vertically Integrated
Liquid (VIL).
Base Reflectivity
Base reflectivity displays echo location and intensity. Location is the direction and distance from
the antenna and the radar depicts intensity in decibels (dBZ). Intensity is determined by
comparing power values between transmitted and reflected waves. The radar uses standard
decibel thresholds of 5 to 80 dBZ to represent different meteorological features. Echoes equal to
or greater than 18 dBZ indicate precipitation, 30 dBZ is enough to indicate possible
thunderstorms, and 50 dBZ may indicate severe thunderstorms. These values are not stand-alone
but should be used as a baseline to determine if a system requires further investigation.
Composite reflectivity: Derived from base reflectivity, composite reflectivity displays the highest
detected reflectivity values with the highest resolution. Composite reflectivity allows users to
quickly identify thunderstorms with the most severe dBZ values.

Hook Echo: A hook-shaped pattern on the radar indicates a powerful thunderstorm with a high
potential for producing a tornado. It forms when precipitation wraps around the back of the
storm's rotating updraft, creating an area of higher dBZ surrounded by lower values. The
presence of a hook echo is a key indicator for tornadoes, especially when other signatures of
rotation are also present.

Limitations. Contamination can occur anytime reflectivity products display incorrect data.
Ground clutter will have abnormally high or low reflectivity values which may be confused with
a severe thunderstorm.
The cone of silence causes targets near the antenna to seemingly dissipate due to the limited
range of VCP angles. To view accurate data of storms entering the cone of silence, change the
radar location to the nearest site to remedy this limitation.

Beam Height can be another limitation. Targets at longer distances from the radar can either be
completely hidden if the top of the storm is below the beam, or you may only see the tops of the
storm without getting an accurate depiction of the entire column.
Base Velocity
Base Velocity displays atmospheric motion. Colors depict speed and direction. Cool colors
(green and blue) represent inbound winds whereas hot colors (red, orange, and yellow) represent
outbound winds. The antenna is the reference point for inbound and outbound winds. The
Doppler zero line is a gray line indicating zero velocity. This is where the winds are
perpendicular to the radar beam resulting in no velocity readings.

Velocity Signatures
- Convergence. At lower elevation scans, outbound flow is between the antenna and an
area of inbound flow. This could indicate thunderstorm updrafts, frontal boundaries, or
squall lines.
- Divergence. Inbound flow is between the antenna and an area of outbound flow. At
higher elevation scans, this may indicate the top of a thunderstorm updraft causing
divergence aloft, while at low elevations it appears with thunderstorm downdrafts.

- Gate-to-Gate Shear. This signature indicates storm rotation. It measures the maximum
inbound and outbound velocities associated with a thunderstorm. Shear is computed by
adding the maximum inbound and maximum outbound values. It will appear on the radar
as a small, concentrated area (usually withing 5 miles) of strong outbound winds
completely enclosed by strong inbound winds or vice versa. This shows that within the
cells of a storm there is an area of strong rotation that is likely to produce a funnel cloud
or possibly a tornado. If gate-to-gate shear is observed, it should be prioritized above all
other weather features and requires constant monitoring.

Echo Tops (ET)

The source for Echo tops is base reflectivity, and it does not display cloud tops. It displays the
highest detected height of greater than 18 dBZ. Its primary usage is identifying storms with the
greatest vertical development. It can aid in defining the storm updraft within a storm. Collapsing
echo (tops rapidly falling between scans) indicate potential thunderstorm downbursts. This
occurs where the tops rapidly fall to lower heights.
Vertically integrated liquid (VIL)
Base reflectivity products also derive a VIL image which displays the total amount of liquid
water content in a vertical column. You will use it to locate the most significant storms based on
liquid content. It is a good indicator of a strengthening thunderstorm when values increase
steadily due to the addition of moisture to the column. VIL can also indicate a downburst when
values decrease rapidly.
Velocity-Azimuth Display (VAD) Wind Profile

A radar plot of horizontal winds above a Radar. The display is plotted with height as the vertical
axis and time as the horizontal axis which then depicts the change in wind with time at various
heights. This display is useful for observing local changes in vertical wind shear, increases in
speed shear, and development of nearby jet streams (including low-level jets).

Numerical weather prediction (NWP) models are three-dimensional mathematical projections of
the atmosphere for specific location over time. Numerical models can be a valuable tool in the
forecasting process. Knowing which weather parameters are available on a model and how to

Numerical Weather Model Types
Deterministic models
Deterministic models produce a forecast for a specific value, at a specific time for various
weather parameters. For example, a deterministic model would forecast temperatures of 23 C at
15Z or pressure at 998 mb at 22Z for a specific location. The creation process begins by pulling
in the observed initial state of the atmosphere through various means such as observations,
satellite data, and Skew-T soundings to establish a baseline to produce a computer-generated
forecast to predict the future state of the atmosphere. Limitations exist due to the inability to
gather precise initial conditions because observations are not available at every location. In
CONUS there are hundreds available but other countries or continents can have extremely

limited observed data. With a poor initial data, the model is unable to account for all the data
gaps and can result in an unreliable finished product.
Stochastic models
Stochastic models produce a probability of occurrence for various weather parameters. These
models would forecast a 20% chance of rain, or a 70% chance of wind speeds greater than or
equal to 10KT for a specific location. These models do not offer any insight as to what features
are causing the higher probabilities. Stochastic models will need to be supplemented with other
model or analysis data to determine the cause of the given probability. The creation process is
like deterministic models; however, the algorithm repeats multiple times using different
parameters as well as intaking other deterministic model results. The more deterministic models
that are forecasting for a specific feature, the higher the probability of occurrence will be on a
stochastic model.
Model Verification
Models are an important part of the forecast process; however, they are not infallible. You must
continuously evaluate their performance. You must verify the models to figure out their

accuracy. The process requires comparing the model data to the observed data for the specific
weather parameters you are forecasting for. Operationally the verification process is becoming
more crucial due to the reliance on model data. Model verification has three potential outcomes:
Accurate
The model data matches the observed data, and the information can be used without any
adjustments.
Example: You are looking at model data to determine what the days maximum temperature will
be. The current observation at 15Z has the temperature at 22 degrees C. The model data says that at 15Z
the temperature will be 22 degrees C. This is an accurate temperature forecast, and you can use those
temperatures assuming that all the forecast temperatures will also be accurate.
Nearly Accurate
The model data does not match the observed data, but the differences are minor, and the
information can be used after making slight adjustments.
Example: You are looking at model data to determine what the pressure will be 6 hours from
now. The current observation at 15Z has the pressure at 29.93. The model data says that at 15Z
the pressure will be 29.94. This could be considered a nearly accurate pressure forecast, and you
can use the pressure values after making slight adjustments. If the model vs. observed pressure
was 00.01 too high, you can assume all forecast pressure values will also be off by the same
amount. Looking 6 hours ahead, the model is forecasting pressure to be 30.01, you could
confidently forecast the actual pressure to be 30.00 after adjusting the values.
Inaccurate
The model data does not mirror the observed data, and the differences make the model
unreliable. If a model is deemed inaccurate, you would find a different model that is more
accurate.

Example: You are looking at model data to determine what the wind speed and direction will be
in 3 hours. The current observation is reporting 35006KT. The model data is forecasting
22012KT. This would be inaccurate, and you should not use this model to forecast wind values
based on the large difference between the observed and model data.

1000-500mb Thickness Model
Thickness is the vertical distance between two constant pressure surfaces. The distance between
1,000 and 500 mb quantifies the dynamic atmosphere. Forecast models assume the surface is
1,000 mb. Forecast models use thickness to depict the average temperature of a layer. High
thickness values indicate warmer air while low thickness values indicate colder air. Thickness
isopleths are lines of equal thickness like isotherms are lines of equal temperature. The thickness
lines combine to reveal a gradient separating warm and cold air that will resemble temperature
gradients.
Thickness gradients depict areas of thermal advection. Models depict thermal advection in areas
with a tight thermal (thickness) gradient and a layer's isobaric or contour gradient. You find
advection by determining the wind flow shown by the isobaric gradient. When two successive
isobars intersect two successive thickness lines, they form an advection box. The box's size
indicates the advection's intensity. Large boxes are weak advection while small boxes are strong
advection. Wind flow also determines the type of advection. Flow from higher to lower thickness
values indicate a southerly flow and equates to warm air advection (WAA). This is common on
the east side of low-pressure centers. Flow from lower to higher thickness values indicate a
northerly flow and equates to cold air advection (CAA) which is most common on the east side
of high-pressure centers.
Models infer the location of the Polar front jet centered within the tightest thickness gradient,
paralleling the 540 decameter (dam) thickness line. The thickness lines also infer air mass
boundaries. The 528-dam line is the boundary between arctic and polar air representing the edge
of the Arctic high meaning values less than 528-dam is arctic air. The 540-dam line is the
rain/snow delineation line and represents the boundary between polar and modified-polar air.
The 570-dam line is the boundary between modified polar and tropical air. Values greater than
570 dam is tropical air.
Lastly, warm and cold fronts are on the warm side of the tightest thickness (thermal) gradient.
Warm air advection is poleward of warm fronts and cold air advection is poleward of cold fronts.
Cold and Warm Fronts always lie in pressure troughs either extending from the low-pressure
center or from the triple point if it is a mature or decaying wave. Occluded fronts will extend
from the triple point through the thickness (thermal) ridge on the northeast side of the low-
pressure center.

- Trend Identification: Observing the changes in individual variables over time (e.g.,

- Correlation Analysis: Examining the relationships between different variables (e.g.,

- Event Prediction: Using observed trends and correlations to forecast future weather

Workplace Operation Impacts & P.A.C.E
P.A.C.E. Planning
The P.A.C.E. framework was developed to ensure there is no single point of failure in any
military operation. This framework is used in nearly every aspect of military operations from
communication networks, aircraft missions, and training exercises. The goal of this section is to
identify each phase of a P.A.C.E. plan, and where each phase would be used in a scenario.

The Primary plan is the standard way your unit operates. This plan accounts for multitudes of
variables and remains viable so long as any alterations to the plan are minor. There should be
little to no risk involved in the primary plan, any risks will be well mitigated.
The Alternate plan is the "second best" option for the unit or operation in question. The
Alternate plan is expected to accomplish mission priorities from the primary plan and is only put
into effect when an unexpected event takes place, requiring a shift in operations that result in the
primary plan being unusable.
The Contingency plan is the third option for the unit or operation in question, and is reserved to
account for large, unexpected changes to both the primary and alternate plans. The contingency
plan is expected to accomplish mission priorities where able and eliminate risk where able.
The Emergency plan is the "last resort" for the unit or operation in question. This plan is
reserved for instances where accomplishing mission priorities or saving resources is the most
important task at hand. The emergency plan is only exercised if the primary, alternate, and
contingency plans have all been rendered unusable. Risk is inherently high with the emergency
plan and should only be used if necessary.
PACE Example:
Communications Mission: Sending a message to a friend.
1. Primary: Send them a text message
2. Alternate: Call them

3. Contingency: Contact someone who knows how to get ahold of them
4. Emergency: Physically travel to the friend to relay the message in person
Notice the primary plan is easy, quick, and effective, making the primary plan the best option for
the mission.
In this scenario, assume you attempt to text, but someone changed the language of your keyboard
to a language you do not speak. Texting your friend is no longer an option, so you must use an
alternate plan to get ahold of them. Calling your friend accomplishes the same goal, with minor
setbacks and inconveniences but is the next best option for accomplishing the mission.
Next, assume that you call but their phone goes straight to voicemail. You must now enact the
contingency plan. You would contact someone who is usually aware of the location and/or
activities of your friend. You contact that person, but they have not heard from your friend in a
few days. The contingency plan has now failed, and you must take emergency action to
accomplish the mission by whatever means necessary.
You decide to physically travel to their house and make direct contact with them. You get the
message to them making your emergency plan the best and last resort option to complete the
mission.

Coastal / Oceanic Missions
Coastal and Oceanic missions include any mission having to do with coastal or open water areas.
These missions are like ground mission in that they care more about surface impacts rather than

Coastal Missions
Coastal missions involve a broad range of operations including maritime law enforcement, port

Key Mission Areas:
- Maritime Law Enforcement: Impeding drug smuggling, human trafficking, and

- Ports, Waterways, & Coastal Security (PWCS): Protecting the Maritime

- Defense Readiness: Conducting military operations, port security, and providing support

- Aids to Navigation (ATON): Maintaining lighthouses, buoys, and Vessel Traffic

Oceanic Missions
Oceanic missions involve broad maritime operations like sea control, power projection,

combating illegal fishing and transnational crime.
Key Mission Areas:
- Sea Control & Power Projection: Dominating sea lanes, launching air/ground attacks
from the sea, and maintaining presence.
- Deterrence: Preventing conflict through visible strength, including nuclear deterrence.
- Maritime Security: Combating piracy, drug trafficking, and enforcing fisheries laws.

Rules of Engagement (ROE) and Course of Action (COA)
Rules of Engagement (ROE)
Rules of Engagement are directives issued by competent military authority that specify the
circumstances and limitations under which forces will initiate or continue missions. ROE are
legal, policy-driven constraints on military action.
Key characteristics:
- ROE defines when, where, and how force can be used. It may impose restrictions on
certain weapons, tactics, or targets.
- ROE ensures that military actions align with domestic and international law, as well as
national policy goals.
- ROE can change depending on the operational environment. They can be very restrictive
in peace operations but less so during high-intensity conflict.
- While ROE helps achieve political and military objectives by controlling escalation, they
do not dictate how to achieve the mission. They only set the boundaries.
Course of Action (COA)

A mission Course of Action is the broad, potential solution a military commander develops to
achieve the mission objective. It is a comprehensive plan detailing the resources, tactics, and
movements of forces to accomplish the mission.
The METOC role in the planning process is crucial for mission planners to adjust their COA if
unfavorable conditions exist for the current planned time or location of the mission. Effective
and timely communication is of upmost importance when forecasting for missions. The
information you deliver can completely alter the current mission plan or even cancel the mission
entirely. It is important to note that your role in the planning process DOES NOT include giving
suggestions or opinions on COAs. Your role is to provide the requested information and any
follow-up information the mission planner might need to adjust the COA of the mission
accordingly.
Key characteristics:
- A COA is an action plan designed to solve a military problem.
- Mission planners may develop multiple COAs during the planning process, evaluating
and comparing them before one is approved.
- The COA is developed based on the commander's intent, the mission statement, and
battlefield analysis.
- The planning process includes developing a COA that can adapt to changing
circumstances as well as account for unplanned contingencies.
ROE and COA Interaction
The relationship between COA and ROE is a constant consideration during military planning.
COA development is limited by ROE. When developing a COA, planners must ensure that all
proposed actions are permissible under the ROE. The existence of strict ROE might force a
commander to develop a less aggressive or more politically sensitive COA.
For example, peace operations with very restrictive ROE will result in a different COA than a
high-intensity combat scenario with more permissive ROE. Tactical feedback can change
ROE. Input from tactical-level commanders executing a COA can inform mission planners if the
current ROE is unworkable or too restrictive. This can lead to an update of the ROE, which in
turn might alter the approved COA.

### 5B. 5-Day Forecast

Source: Complete_Curriculum_Text.pdf p. 205
Introduction to the 5-Day Forecast
The purpose of the 5-Day forecast is to provide a basic weather outlook to the customer. This
product is used for planning purposes only but often serves as a great tool for commanders and
base personnel to plan future events. The goal is to create a product that is as accurate as possible
with the understanding that the further out the forecast is, the more variables can change. Proper
forecasting techniques are also important as this will help lead to accuracy for the entire forecast
period.
5-Day Forecast Formatting
The 5-Day format is very similar to civilian products you may have seen before on the local
news channel or weather apps. This product is a picture-based and will include these elements:
expected weather, sky condition, visibility, temperature, wind, and stoplight charts to
communicate mission conditions. Each day of the forecast is broken into 12-hour blocks for a
total of 10 sections of information included on the 5-day. Use the following information when
creating and briefing the 5-day forecast.
Present Weather: Select the predominant type of weather expected throughout the 12-hour
window, or select the worst weather expected.
Sky Condition: Report the predominant sky condition for the 12-hour period to include the
amount of coverage, and the bases of clouds. If your sky condition for the time is Clear -
Scattered, you do not need to include the height of clouds. Bases are only reported with Broken -
Overcast skies. Use the following verbiage on the 5-day to convey sky cover.
- Clear: CLR
- Partly Cloudy: FEW-SCT
- Mostly Cloudy: BKN
- Overcast: BKN-OVC
Visibility: Report the predominant visibility in statute miles with 10 representing unrestricted
visibility. Remember that if you have any restriction to visibility, you must have present weather
reported. If you have visibility restrictions in one of the blocks, report the lowest expected
visibility.
Temperature: You will report the highest and lowest temperatures in Fahrenheit for the entire
24-hour period. This is the only part of the 5-day that is not broken up into 12-hour blocks.
Wind: Report the predominant wind speed and direction for every 6-hours. Include a Gust if
applicable. Remember to round your wind direction to the nearest 10 degrees. For training
purposes, round your wind speeds to the nearest 5KT.

Identifying Mission Impacts and Thresholds
The mission condition portion of the 5-day is broken down by the hour for the entire 5-day. This
is regularly the first form of communication you give to mission planners, so breaking down the
impacts on an hourly basis will give them the most amount of information. Only include the
stoplight chart and mission conditions for the mission you are supporting.
If there are marginal or unfavorable conditions in this section, ensure the conditions in the above
information is consistent with the mission condition. When briefing this section, be sure to
include specifically what weather parameter is causing marginal or unfavorable mission impacts.

### 5E. Watches, Warnings, and Advisories (WWA's)

Source: Complete_Curriculum_Text.pdf p. 220
Introduction to WWA's
The goal of this objective will be to show you have the capability to identify the necessary
weather watch, warning, and/or advisory criteria based on provided forecast products. To
successfully demonstrate competency on this topic, you will be given information through
lecture, a series of questions and tasks designed to display your ability to correctly apply
different thresholds and timelines to forecasted weather events. These foundational concepts will
be measured with a Progress Check (PC) at the end of the objective and must be passed to
continue onto the next objective. Follow your instructor's guidance as they provide information
and instruction for completing all requirements necessary to pass this objective.
Characteristics and Threshold
Criteria
WWA criteria refers to the list of weather events that require WWA input. Some are forecasted
events, while others are only issued upon observation. With the goal of WWA criteria being
resource protection, the specific criteria may vary between units based on their individual needs
and resource thresholds.
Watch
A watch means that hazardous weather is possible. A watch is used when the risk of a hazardous
weather event has increased significantly, but its occurrence, location or timing is still uncertain.
It is intended to provide enough lead time so those who need to set plans in motion can do so.
Warning
A warning is issued when a hazardous weather event occurs or is imminent. A warning means
weather conditions pose a threat to life or property. People in the path of the storm need to take
protective action.
Advisory
An advisory is issued when a less serious hazardous weather event is occurring or is imminent.
Advisories cause inconvenience and if caution is not exercised, could lead to situations that may
threaten life or property.
Timing
The timing of WWA notices are crucial for successful resource and personnel protection. There
are several timing thresholds to consider when forecasting and issuing WWAs.
Issue time: This is the time you submit the WWA to external agencies.
Valid times: The forecast period for the WWA criteria to occur with beginning and ending times.
Each WWA has its own valid time.

Desired Lead Time (DLT): The minimum required time before the criteria is forecast that you
must issue the WWA to alert external agencies to take protective measures.
Lead Time: This is the time between issued time and the first occurrence of the forecast event.
You subtract the issue time from the first occurrence time to figure out the lead time.
- Positive Lead Time: If the first occurrence time is after the issue time, you have a positive
lead time.
- Negative Lead Time: If the first occurrence time is before the issue time, you have a
negative lead time.
Timing Error: Timing error is the time between the forecast valid time and the actual time of the
criterion's first occurrence. Subtract the forecast time from the time of occurrence and if the
event occurred later than forecast, then the timing error is positive. If the event occurred before
the forecast valid time, then your timing error is negative.
False Alarm: False alarms are when a WWA is issued but the forecasted event never occurred.
Required Not Issued (RNI): An RNI is when there was no WWA criteria forecasted and a WWA
was never issued, but one of the WWA criteria occurs. This is the least desirable outcome for
forecasters. With a major priority of our job being resource protection, having a significant
weather event occur without any agency having warning could result in significant damage to
resources or injury to personnel.

Horizontal Consistency
All issued WWAs must be horizontally consistent with all forecast and observed products we
create. These include hourly observations, 5-Day forecasts, Mission Execution Forecasts,
Terminal Aerodrome Forecasts, Mission Briefs, and any other product you are required to create.
Failing to maintain horizontal consistency between all your products could result in
misinformation while mission planners attempt to adjust for weather considerations. If your
products vary in timing or severity of weather events could result in a loss of credibility to your
customer. If the products have differing information, this could convey lack of attention to detail
that will affect the confidence the customer has in your ability to provide useful or accurate
information.

Flight Weather Brief Format
Part 1: Local Surface Data
Box 1 - Date: The date of the mission
Box 2 - Mission Name: The name given to this mission
Box 3 - Departure Point / Estimated Time of Departure: The station ICAO where you will begin

Box 4/5 - Runway Temperature / Dewpoint: The forecasted temperature and dewpoint for the

Box 6 - Altimeter: The forecasted altimeter value in INS for the time listed in box 3.
Box 7 - Visibility: The forecasted visibility in SM for the time listed in box 3.
Box 8 - Mission Condition: Based on the mission thresholds, input the mission condition for the
start of the mission in this box. If you input marginal or unfavorable, this would require
justification in box 13.
Box 9 - Surface Winds: Forecasted surface winds for the time listed in box 3.
Box 10 - Climb Winds: For aviation missions, input the wind data for surface winds, 5,000 ft
winds, and 10,000 ft winds.
Box 11 - Local WWAs: If there are any active WWAs that will still be valid for the time listed
in box 3, or if you are forecasting conditions that could potentially lead to issuing a WWA for
that time, input the WWA in this box.
Box 13 - Remarks: If there is any other data you feel is important or that you feel the mission
planner should know, input that information in plain language in this box. This box will also
house your justification for any marginal or unfavorable mission conditions.

Part 2: Enroute & Mission Data
Box 14 - Flight Level Winds / Temperature: Based on the given flight level for your mission,
input the temperature and wind data in this section for what the pilot can expect at their flight
level.
Box 15 - Space Weather: Navigate to the space weather bulletin and input the date and time
window of the space impacts, the severity of the impact and the type.
Box 16 - Solar / Lunar: Navigate to the solar/lunar data on Bifrost and access the ICAO closest
to your mission location. Ensure you gather data for the correct date and input the solar / lunar
data onto your brief in Zulu time. If there is no ICAO located near your mission location,
navigate to the US Naval Observatory Solar / Lunar data and use the latitude / longitude of your
mission location to gather this data. This data is useful for every mission type to determine the
best course of action if any amount of stealth is required.
Box 17 - Clouds at Flight Level: Forecast the amount of cloud coverage the pilot can expect at
their flight level.
Box 18 - Obscurations Restricting Visibility: At the pilot's flight level, input if there is any
expected obscurations they should be aware of. If you have already indicated the presence of
clouds at flight level, you do not need to put that in this section. This box is for any other
phenomena that could restrict visibility.
Box 19 - Minimum Ceiling Location: Along the route of the mission, input the lowest ceiling if
applicable and general location of them. If no clouds are forecast, you can put N/A in this
section.

Box 20 - Maximum Cloud Tops: Along the route of the mission, input the highest cloud tops
expected and the general location of those tops. If no clouds are forecast, you can put N/A in this
section.
Box 21 - Minimum Freezing Level: Along the route of the mission, input the lowest freezing
level height expected and the general location of the low freezing level.
Box 22 - Thunderstorms: First, input the timeframe of the hazard chart you used to gather
thunderstorm data. Next, indicate whether there are no thunderstorms along the mission route, if
the thunderstorms are covering an area, or if the thunderstorms are in a line. Then mark the
appropriate amount of coverage based on the hazard charts and include the general location of
the thunderstorms.
Box 23 - Turbulence: First, input the timeframe of the hazard chart you used to gather
turbulence data. Next, indicate the type, severity, layers, and location of forecasted turbulence
along the mission route.
Box 24 - Icing: First, input the timeframe of the hazard chart you used to gather icing data. Next,
indicate the type, severity, layers, and location of forecasted icing along the mission route.
Box 25 - Precipitation: If there is any forecasted precipitation along your mission route, input the
time frame in the first box, then indicate the type as either rain or snow, then mark the intensity
of the forecasted precipitation. Last, add the general location precipitation is expected.
Part 3: Mission Execution Forecast
Box 26 - Destination / Alternate Destinations: These boxes will contain the location / ICAO of
the target area for your mission. If your mission requires multiple locations, use the alternate
destination boxes to give mission planners that information.
Box 27 - Valid Time: Input the valid times for the mission in these boxes in Zulu time. These are
the times that the mission is expected to operate at its target location.
Box 28 - Surface Winds: These are the winds that are expected at your target location during the
valid times. If the valid time covers a significant period, input either the average wind data if
nothing is expected to impact operations or put the worst wind conditions expected during the
mission window.

Box 29 - Visibility / Weather: Input the forecasted surface visibility and any weather expected at
your target location within the valid time. If no weather is expected, input NSW.
Box 30 - Cloud Layers: For aviation missions, input the cloud layers in this box without using
the summation principle. This should alert the pilots to the total picture of cloud coverage above
the target location.
Box 31 - Altimeter: Input the lowest altimeter expected for your target location during the
mission valid time.
Box 32 - Maximum Temperature: Input the maximum expected temperature is Fahrenheit and
Celsius for the target location during the valid times.
Box 33 - Minimum Temperature: Input the minimum expected temperature is Fahrenheit and
Celsius for the target location during the valid times.
Part 4 & 5: Comments / Briefing Record
Box 35 - Remarks: If there is any information you feel the mission planners need to be aware of
that was not included in the brief, input that information here.
Box 36 - Weather Briefed Time: The date and time in Zulu you delivered the brief.
Box 37 - Briefer Initials: Input your initials in this block.
Box 38 - Person Receiving Brief Initials: Input either the rank and name of the individual you
brief or their initials if that is the information they give you.
